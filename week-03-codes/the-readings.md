## 1. IFLA (2012)

**IFLA code of ethics for librarians and other information workers (full version)**

> International Federation of Library Associations and Institutions. (2012). IFLA code of ethics for librarians and other information workers (full version). IFLA. (Approved by the IFLA Governing Board, August 2012; prepared by L. Garcia-Febo, A. Hustad, H. Rösch, P. Sturges, & A. Vallotton for the FAIFE working group)

**Find it.** https://www.ifla.org/g/faife/professional-codes-of-ethics-for-librarians/ · PDF: https://www.ifla.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/assets/faife/publications/IFLA%20Code%20of%20Ethics%20-%20Long_0.pdf · Deposit copy: https://repository.ifla.org/items/6c3d64ed-4eb8-45e4-bb60-9e178aa94cbf

**In the course project.** `IFLA Code of Ethics for Librarians and Other Information Workers Long Version.pdf`

This is a model code, offered "for the consideration of Library and Information Associations when creating or revising their own codes." It does not replace national codes and states that "full compliance with this code is not expected." That is a design choice for a document addressing associations in radically different legal and political environments, not a weakness.

The preamble does the philosophical work. It asserts that "librarianship is, in its very essence, an ethical activity embodying a value-rich approach to professional work with information," and that because information service serves "social, cultural and economic well-being," librarians "have social responsibility." It grounds this in Article 19 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the right to "seek, receive and impart information and ideas in any media and regardless of frontiers." From information rights it derives a striking duty: librarians must "develop a principled critique of relevant law and be prepared to advise and, if appropriate, advocate the improvement of both the substance and administration of laws." The code obliges you to criticize the legal order you work within.

Six clauses follow. **(1) Access to information** makes access the core mission and rejects restriction "most particularly through censorship whether by states, governments, or religious or civil society institutions," urging free-of-cost service and international web accessibility compliance. **(2) Responsibilities towards individuals and society** lists a long non-discrimination catalogue including immigration and asylum-seeking status, gender identity, and sexual orientation, commits to language minorities, requires information literacy instruction, and hedges that protection of minors must not "impact on the information rights of adults." **(3) Privacy, secrecy and transparency** requires confidentiality beyond the original transaction and endorses whistleblowing in the public interest as a justified breach of it. **(4) Open access and intellectual property** supports open access, open source, and open licenses, declares a "professional duty to advocate for exceptions and limitations to copyright restrictions for libraries," insists licenses must not override statutory library exceptions, and calls for limited terms and a public domain that stays public. **(5) Neutrality, personal integrity and professional skills** is the contested clause, and it also requires that selection, organisation, preservation, and dissemination policies be published. **(6) Colleague and employer/employee relationship** covers fair treatment, anti-discrimination in employment, and equal pay for comparable work. This is the most expansive statement of professional obligation in the course, reaching labor conditions, legal advocacy, and whistleblower protection, and also the most internally strained.

:::{tip} Why it's here
It is the global counterweight to the American documents and the clearest delivery of Objective O3. It is also the only Block 1 reading that treats your rights *as a worker* as an ethical matter, which will matter more to your career than most of what you read this semester.
:::

> "Librarians and other information workers are strictly committed to neutrality and an unbiased stance regarding collection, access and service. Neutrality results in the most balanced collection and the most balanced access to information achievable." (IFLA Code of Ethics, full version, Clause 5)

## 2. ALA (2021)

**ALA code of ethics**

> American Library Association. (2021). ALA code of ethics. American Library Association. (Adopted 1939; amended June 30, 1981; June 28, 1995; January 22, 2008; and June 29, 2021)

**Find it.** https://www.ala.org/tools/ethics

**In the course project.** `ALACode of Ethics.pdf`

Nine principles and a framing preamble, maintained by ALA's Committee on Professional Ethics. The preamble makes three claims worth marking: that library workers "significantly influence or control the selection, organization, preservation, and dissemination of information," a frank acknowledgment of power rather than a claim to neutrality about it; that in "a political system grounded in an informed citizenry" the profession is "explicitly committed to intellectual freedom and the freedom of access to information"; and that ethical dilemmas "occur when values are in conflict," the principles being broad statements that "cannot and do not dictate conduct to cover particular situations." The Code tells you in its opening lines that it will not decide your hard cases.

The nine: highest level of service through equitable policies and "accurate, unbiased, and courteous responses"; upholding intellectual freedom and resisting "all efforts to censor library resources"; protecting user privacy and confidentiality; respecting intellectual property rights while advocating balance between users and rights holders; treating colleagues with respect and advocating conditions that safeguard employee rights; not advancing private interests at others' expense; distinguishing personal convictions from professional duties; striving for excellence and developing colleagues; and, ninth, affirming the dignity of every person while working to dismantle biases, confront inequity and oppression, enhance diversity and inclusion, and advance racial and social justice.

The ninth rewards close reading, and not only because it is newest. Its verbs are active and institutional: recognize, dismantle, confront, enhance, advance. Its final clause names "allocation of resources and spaces," meaning budgets and rooms, not sentiments. It applies to "profession, and associations," making ALA itself an object of the principle. Read against Knox, it is best understood not as a departure from intellectual freedom but as codification of what the profession had been arguing about since at least 2018. Read Principle 1's "accurate, unbiased, and courteous responses" alongside Principle 7's separation of conviction from duty: these are ALA's version of what IFLA calls neutrality, but ALA locates the requirement at the level of the individual worker's conduct toward users, not the institution's stance toward the world. That is a defensible reconciliation of Principles 7 and 9, and one you should be able to say out loud.

:::{tip} Why it's here
You will be held to this document professionally in the United States. It is also the shortest text in the block, and it contains the specific conflict the Block 1 scenario is built to surface. Its brevity is deceptive: nine sentences that took eighty-two years and four amendments to reach.
:::

> "We affirm the inherent dignity and rights of every person. We work to recognize and dismantle systemic and individual biases; to confront inequity and oppression; to enhance diversity and inclusion; and to advance racial and social justice in our libraries, communities, profession, and associations through awareness, advocacy, education, collaboration, services, and allocation of resources and spaces." (ALA Code of Ethics, Principle 9, adopted June 29, 2021)

## 3. ALA (2025)

**Copyright: An interpretation of the Code of Ethics**

> American Library Association. (2025). Copyright: An interpretation of the Code of Ethics. American Library Association. (Adopted July 1, 2014; amended January 29, 2019, and June 30, 2025)

**Find it.** https://www.ala.org/tools/ethics/copyright

**In the course project.** `ALACopyright_ An Interpretation of the Code of Ethics.pdf`

This document elaborates Principle 4. Its argument is that copyright and libraries share a purpose, so libraries are not adversaries of copyright but its intended beneficiaries and practical engine. Copyright "was established to encourage people to share their created works" by granting exclusive rights, and the limits on those rights, including fair use and limited term, "are necessary to protect creators' rights while also preserving freedom of information and freedom of expression." Libraries spend billions on copyrighted materials and lend billions of items, serving copyright's "use and access" purpose.

It sets out the framework plainly: exclusive rights to reproduce, create derivative works, distribute, and publicly perform or display (17 U.S.C. § 106); fair use and its four factors (§ 107); first sale, permitting borrowing, lending, selling, and transferring a lawfully acquired copy (§ 109); and the library, archive, and nonprofit educational exception (§ 108) covering limited user copies, preservation and replacement copies, accessibility copies, and educational performance or display.

The duties it derives are more demanding than they first appear. Institutions must maintain policies aligned with ethical duties, mission, and law, and ensure staff know the copyright rules affecting their services. Workers must stay current, analyze fair use scenarios "with good judgment and strategies to reduce risk," do outreach, and provide information about the law while avoiding legal advice. Two duties deserve special attention. Workers "should be able to explain to users any restrictions on how they can use content based on digital-licensing agreements that may exceed those covered by copyright law," obliging you to tell patrons where your license is more restrictive than the statute. And "if there is an unfair balance between the rights holders and the public's access to information, library workers should speak up," engaging rights holders and legislators. Advocacy is framed as an ethical requirement, not an option. The June 30, 2025 revision is itself an object lesson: an Interpretation adopted in 2014, amended in 2019 and again in 2025, while Principle 4 has not changed a word.

:::{tip} Why it's here
Nowhere else in Block 1 do you watch a code being *applied* rather than stated, and this one bridges the abstract commitments of the two codes to the licensing and access problems of Blocks 2 and 4. It also completes the argument the Manifesto opened in Week 1 about digital content on reasonable terms.
:::

> "Libraries have both the opportunity and the duty to maintain this balance." (ALA, *Copyright: An Interpretation of the Code of Ethics*, amended June 30, 2025)
