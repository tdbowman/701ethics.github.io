## 🗝️ Key Concepts

| Term | What it means |
|------|---------------|
| **Model code** | IFLA's self-description: offered for adaptation by national associations rather than enforcement, explicitly not expecting full compliance. Its authority is persuasive and citational. |
| **Social responsibility** | The claim, in IFLA's preamble and ALA Principle 9, that because information service serves social, cultural, and economic well-being, information workers bear obligations beyond service delivery. |
| **Article 19 (UDHR)** | The right to seek, receive, and impart information regardless of frontiers, cited by both the IFLA Code and Knox as grounding intellectual freedom as a human right rather than a professional preference. |
| **Neutrality (IFLA Clause 5)** | A commitment to an unbiased stance in collection, access, and service, defended as producing the most balanced collection achievable. Compare Knox's evidence that this is not an official ALA value. |
| **Principle 7 / Principle 9 tension** | ALA's requirement to separate conviction from duty, set against its requirement to confront inequity. The preamble anticipates it: "ethical dilemmas occur when values are in conflict." |
| **Principled critique of law** | IFLA's preamble duty to critique relevant law and advocate its improvement, making legal compliance a floor rather than a ceiling. |
| **Whistleblowing (IFLA Clause 3)** | The recognition that exposing misconduct, corruption, and crime may justify breaching confidentiality in the public interest, placed deliberately beside user-privacy commitments. |
| **Fair use (§ 107)** | Four factors, case by case, with no safe formula: purpose and character, nature of the work, amount and substantiality, market effect. |
| **First sale (§ 109) and the § 108 exception** | The doctrines making lending and preservation copying possible for physical items, whose weakness in digital licensing is why the Manifesto's "reasonable terms" clause exists. |
| **License overriding statute** | Where a contract restricts uses copyright law would allow. ALA's Interpretation obliges you to explain this to users; IFLA Clause 4 obliges you to negotiate against it. |

All terms from all sixteen weeks are collected in the [Glossary](../glossary.md).

## 💬 Annotation & Discussion Prompts

These are written for **Perusall**. Each one points at something specific and each one is arguable, which is the point: annotation credit comes from advancing a conversation, not from summarizing a paragraph.

1. Place IFLA Clause 5 beside ALA Principle 9 and annotate one. Are these incompatible claims, or is IFLA predicating neutrality of the *service transaction* while ALA predicates justice of the *institution*? If the second reading works, what practical difference remains?
2. Annotate Principle 7. Construct a case in which following it requires violating Principle 9, then decide which you would follow and what you would tell your staff.
3. The ALA preamble says the principles "cannot and do not dictate conduct." Is a code that refuses to decide cases performing a useful function, or giving the profession the appearance of ethics without the cost?
4. IFLA obliges librarians to "develop a principled critique of relevant law." Name one U.S. information law or licensing practice you believe a librarian is ethically obliged to criticize, and say what criticizing it would cost you in a job.
5. The copyright Interpretation requires explaining to users when a digital license is more restrictive than copyright law. Most libraries do not do this. Is that a resourcing problem, a communications problem, or an ethical failure?
6. IFLA Clause 3 endorses whistleblowing as a justified breach of confidentiality while requiring that user data not be shared beyond the original transaction. What principle distinguishes the two cases?
7. Both codes predate routine generative AI use. Pick one clause from either that will not survive the next decade unamended, and argue for the amendment you would propose.

## 🔗 Connections

- **Scenario:** The **Consequences** and **Contemplation** stages ask you to justify a decision and reflect on it, and codes are the vocabulary of justification. Before you play, decide which of the nine ALA principles you will lead with publicly and which you are prepared to be seen subordinating, because the scenario will make you subordinate one. If your defense of keeping a challenged book rests on Principle 2 alone, a challenger who cites Principle 9 back at you on behalf of a different community will have an argument you cannot answer.
- **Final essay:** Part 2 asks for two or three actionable commitments and one unresolved tension. Commitments should trace to specific clauses rather than general good will, and the IFLA Clause 5 / ALA Principle 9 conflict is a genuinely unresolved tension you can hold honestly. The Interpretation's duty to disclose license restrictions is a ready-made commitment with an obvious algorithmic analogue: telling users when a system's ranking, rather than your collection, is what they are seeing.
- **Threads forward:** Block 2 develops ALA Principle 3 and IFLA Clause 3 into privacy and data ethics. Block 3 develops Principle 9 into DEI practice. Block 4 tests both codes against AI. Block 5 asks you to synthesize the whole into a personal professional ethics, which is what these codes were built to help you do.

## 📚 Going Deeper

Optional, and genuinely optional. These are also good places to find a final-essay angle.

- ALA, "Interpretations of the Library Bill of Rights": https://www.ala.org/advocacy/intfreedom/librarybill/interpretations — Skim the titles to see where the profession found seven articles insufficient; each Interpretation marks a real dispute.
- *International Review of Information Ethics* (open access): https://informationethics.ca/index.php/irie — The main peer-reviewed venue for information ethics as a field, with strong non-U.S. representation.
- Foskett, D. J. (1962). *The creed of a librarian: No politics, no religion, no morals*. Library Association. — The origin of the neutrality doctrine Knox traces, read with her caveat that Foskett described the reference interview.
- Mill, J. S. (1859). *On liberty*. — The four arguments Knox summarizes are the backbone of both codes' anti-censorship clauses. Public domain, and shorter than you expect.
- U.S. Copyright Office, "Fair Use Index": https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/ — Searchable summaries of actual fair use decisions, worth more to your judgment than any four-factor checklist.
