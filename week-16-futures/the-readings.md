## 1. Baer (2025)

**Investigating the "feeling rules" of generative AI and imagining alternative futures**

> Baer, A. (2025, July 16). Investigating the "feeling rules" of generative AI and imagining alternative futures. In the Library with the Lead Pipe. https://www.inthelibrarywiththeleadpipe.org/2025/ai-feeling-rules/

**Find it.** https://www.inthelibrarywiththeleadpipe.org/2025/ai-feeling-rules/ (open access, CC-BY 4.0)

**In the course project.** `Investigating the "Feeling Rules" of Generative AI and Imagining Alternative Futures – In the Library with the Lead Pipe.pdf`

Note the author's name: **Andrea Baer**, not "Baier." The Fall 2025 list carried that misspelling and it is corrected for Fall 2026. Get it right in your reference list.

Baer opens at the April 2025 ACRL conference, where Ruha Benjamin's keynote framed the theme as "a battle over who gets to own the future, which is also a battle over who gets to think their own thoughts." Baer registers the dissonance between that keynote and a program dominated by sessions on integrating generative AI into public services. She then places alongside it the April 23, 2025 executive order establishing a White House Task Force on AI Education, observing that its language about demystifying AI and preparing confident participants in an AI-assisted workforce is nearly indistinguishable from the language of well-intentioned academic librarians. That resemblance is her opening provocation.

The analytic core is Hochschild's concept. Feeling rules are social norms prescribing which feelings are appropriate to have and express; they are typically established by those with power and internalized by those they govern. Baer extends this from individual interactions to professional discourse. For generative AI the rules run: be enthusiastic, be optimistic, treat change as inevitable, and understand reluctance as irrational fear to be overcome. She documents the surrounding culture — "AI anxiety" articles whose prescribed remedy is more AI use, including a PwC executive advising that employees "must lean into the technology" — then asks the question the article turns on: what if those emotional responses are in large part deeply intelligent, an accurate recognition of how these tools are built and what harms they already do?

The second half is a rhetorical analysis of AI literacy frameworks: Barnard College's Bloom's-taxonomy-based framework, where "Use and Apply AI" precedes "Analyze and Evaluate" and the top level is "Create AI"; Stanford Teaching Commons', where functional literacy precedes ethical literacy; and, at greatest length, ACRL President Leo Lo's 2025 "AI Literacy: A Guide for Academic Libraries" and the March 2025 draft ACRL *AI Competencies for Academic Library Workers*. Her recurring finding is that these documents define AI literacy as predicated on use — Lo's definition is "the ability to understand, use, and think critically about AI technologies" — so non-use is unavailable as a literate position. She reads the draft competencies' *dispositions* closely, noting that appropriately disposed library workers "Are open to the potential of responsible human-AI collaboration to unlock a future of greater equity and inclusion," and asks what that does to a colleague who has concluded, on evidence, the opposite. Are they then AI illiterate?

The alternative draws on Charles Logan's proposal for a more heterogeneous approach leaving room for non-use and for mapping GenAI's social, technical, and political-economic ecologies; on Joel Blechinger's question of whether AI literacy claims reflect a professional investment in "the literacy myth"; and on Sano-Franchini, McIntyre, and Fernandes's *Refusing GenAI in Writing Studies*, which defines refusal as "the range of ways that individuals and/or groups consciously and intentionally choose to refuse GenAI use, when and where we are able to do so" — a stance the authors stress is neither monolithic, nor head-in-the-sand, nor equivalent to banning student use. Baer is generous throughout: she notes the ACRL task force actively solicited and engaged critical feedback, and reads that as evidence that feeling rules are contested and revisable rather than fixed. She closes with open questions for professional conversation and teaching practice, and with Benjamin's reframing of "Who owns the future?" into "Who shares the future?"

:::{tip} Why it's here
Because the final essay asks you to argue with an algorithm, and this is the article that gives you permission to notice how you feel about being scored — plus a scholarly apparatus for treating that feeling as evidence rather than noise. It also models the tone Part 2 should aim for: committed, specific, generous to opponents, unwilling to be managed.
:::

## 2. Dezuanni & Osman (2024)

**IFLA trend report 2024: Facing the future of information with confidence**

> Dezuanni, M., & Osman, K. (2024). IFLA trend report 2024: Facing the future of information with confidence. International Federation of Library Associations and Institutions. https://repository.ifla.org/items/ae4dfcc0-8def-4318-8c4c-7f0507d15609

**Find it.** https://www.ifla.org/wp-content/uploads/ifla-trend-report-2024.pdf (open access)

The Trend Report is a recurring foresight exercise. The 2024 edition was prepared for IFLA by Michael Dezuanni and Kim Osman of the Digital Media Research Centre at Queensland University of Technology, funded by Stichting IFLA Global Libraries, with a foreword by then-IFLA President Vicki McDonald. Its method combines a comprehensive literature review of knowledge and information trends with scenario development exploring possible futures — worth noticing, because scenario methods are designed to produce plural futures rather than a single forecast, and that design choice is itself an argument about how the profession should relate to what is coming.

Seven trends are identified. *Knowledge practices are changing*, with opportunities and challenges for equity in knowledge systems. *AI and other technologies are transforming society*, reshaping how information is created, shared, and used. *Trust is being renegotiated*, with re-establishing trust in government and media treated as a precondition for societal flourishing. *Skills and abilities are becoming more complex*, requiring practical, critical, and digital competencies together. *Digital technologies are unevenly distributed*, so digital inclusion work can close equity gaps. *Information systems are using more resources*, making environmental cost a professional concern. And *people are seeking community connections*, with shared spaces supporting cohesion.

Two features distinguish this from a technology-trend list. Only one of the seven is about technology as such; the others concern equity, trust, competence, distribution, environment, and belonging. And the framing is confidence rather than threat — a deliberate counterweight to the obsolescence narrative Baer describes. Whether that confidence is earned or performed is a fair question, and asking it is a better use of your annotation than summarizing the seven trends back. Note the overlap with UNESCO and ACRL: resource consumption meets UNESCO's Policy Area 5; uneven distribution meets everything you read in Block 2; trust meets Hanell's canaries. Convergence across three bodies using different methods is worth something — but recall Floridi and Cowls's caution that convergence among overlapping institutions is not automatically consensus.

:::{tip} Why it's here
It is the widest-angle lens in the course, and your philosophy statement will read very differently written against a picture of the profession's next decade rather than against this semester. It also best supports a commitment about environmental cost, which few students write and which is well evidenced.
:::

## 3. ACRL Research Planning Cmte. (2026)

**2026 top trends in academic libraries: A review of the trends and issues**

> ACRL Research Planning and Review Committee. (2026). 2026 top trends in academic libraries: A review of the trends and issues. College & Research Libraries News, 87(6). https://crln.acrl.org/index.php/crlnews/article/view/27364

**Find it.** https://crln.acrl.org/index.php/crlnews/article/view/27364 (open access)

Top Trends is produced biennially by ACRL's Research Planning and Review Committee through a literature scan and committee deliberation. Its usefulness is that it names pressures actually being discussed in the field rather than ones a futures methodology generates. The 2026 edition is notably darker than its predecessors, and most of the darkness is fiscal and political.

The eight trends: **federal and state legislative actions**, in which political shifts have reduced grant funding from NSF, NIH, NEH, and IMLS while state-level restrictions on diversity, equity, and inclusion work constrain library operations; **budget cuts and institutional closures**, with the committee reporting that since 2020 roughly three-quarters of academic libraries have experienced a budget cut, alongside program consolidation and mergers driven by enrollment decline; **structural higher education issues**, including deferred maintenance, technical debt, and the demographic enrollment cliff; **AI integration and ethical concerns**, with libraries adopting AI-powered research tools while navigating vendor pressure, transparency gaps, and the need for critical AI literacy instruction; **ADA Title II compliance**, with WCAG 2.1 Level AA now operative for library websites and digital collections; **human resources challenges**, driven by salary stagnation, relocation barriers, erosion of tenure, and political climate; **data culture and data rescue**, with libraries expanding data services and responding to federal data loss through grassroots efforts such as the Data Rescue Project; and **environmental sustainability**, including climate-conscious collection strategies, open access, and alignment with the UN Sustainable Development Goals.

Read the first trend against the ALA IMLS FAQ from Week 13 and you have the same event in two genres: advocacy and environmental scan. Read the fourth against Baer for the week's sharpest test — the report explicitly pairs adoption with vendor pressure and transparency gaps, closer to Baer's position than most professional documents get, and yet the framing remains navigation rather than choice. Read the seventh against Week 15: preservation has become an act of political urgency performed partly by volunteers, which no preservation textbook anticipated.

:::{tip} Why it's here
Because it is the most current, most concrete picture of the conditions your commitments will have to survive, and short enough to read twice. If your professional philosophy cannot be practiced under the conditions this report describes, it is a wish rather than a philosophy.
:::
