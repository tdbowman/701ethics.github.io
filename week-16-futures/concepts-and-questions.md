## 🗝️ Key Concepts

| Term | What it means |
|------|---------------|
| **Feeling rules** | social norms prescribing which feelings are appropriate to have and express, established by those with power and typically internalized by those they govern (Baer 2025, citing Hochschild 1979, 1983). |
| **Emotional labor** | the work of managing one's own feelings to meet an employer's or profession's expectations; the workplace context in which feeling rules operate (Baer 2025). |
| **AI refusal** | the range of ways individuals or groups consciously and intentionally decline generative AI use where able; explicitly not monolithic, not ignorance, and not equivalent to banning student use (Baer 2025, citing Sano-Franchini, McIntyre, & Fernandes 2024). |
| **The inevitability narrative** | the claim that GenAI is the unavoidable future of libraries and rapid adoption the only viable response, situated within an older story that libraries must embrace technological change or become obsolete (Baer 2025). |
| **AI literacy predicated on use** | the structural feature Baer identifies in the Barnard, Stanford, and ACRL frameworks, in which understanding and evaluation follow use, leaving non-use unavailable as a literate position (Baer 2025). |
| **Dispositions** | in competency frameworks, the attitudes a competent practitioner is expected to hold; Baer's target because dispositions statements encode feeling rules as professional requirements (Baer 2025). |
| **Imagination as a resource** | Ruha Benjamin's reframing, quoted by Baer, of imagination not as flights of fancy but as "a resource, a capacity, a muscle" for building alternative futures (Baer 2025, quoting Benjamin 2025). |
| **Scenario development** | the foresight method underpinning the IFLA Trend Report, generating plural possible futures rather than a single forecast (Dezuanni & Osman 2024). |
| **Data rescue** | grassroots and library-led efforts to capture and preserve federal data at risk of removal; named as a 2026 trend by ACRL and linked to the Data Rescue Project. |
| **Technical debt** | accumulated deferred maintenance in systems and infrastructure constraining what an institution can do next; named among the structural pressures in ACRL 2026. |

All terms from all sixteen weeks are collected in the [Glossary](../glossary.md).

## 💬 Annotation & Discussion Prompts

These are written for **Perusall**. Each one points at something specific and each one is arguable, which is the point: annotation credit comes from advancing a conversation, not from summarizing a paragraph.

1. Baer asks what if "AI anxiety" is "in large part deeply intelligent." Annotate that passage with a moment in this course when you suppressed a reservation about a technology because of how it would land. What rule were you following?
2. Mark Lo's definition of AI literacy as "the ability to understand, use, and think critically about AI technologies" and Baer's objection to the middle verb. Write the definition you would defend, and say what it costs to hold it.
3. Annotate the draft ACRL dispositions Baer quotes. Should a competency document specify attitudes at all? Argue it either way.
4. Baer reads the ACRL task force's engagement with critical feedback as evidence that feeling rules are contested rather than fixed. Is her generosity analytically earned, or does it soften an argument that should stay sharp?
5. Only one of the IFLA Trend Report's seven trends is about technology as such. Is that proportion a strength of the report, or a sign it is avoiding the hardest trend?
6. The ACRL report pairs AI adoption with vendor pressure and transparency gaps but still frames the task as navigation rather than choice. Rewrite that trend in one sentence as Baer would.
7. Across all five blocks, choose the reading that most directly contradicts another you found persuasive. Annotate where they collide and say what you now believe, without splitting the difference.

## 🔗 Connections

- **Scenario:** Your capstone has already produced a philosophy label for you. Baer gives you the tool to notice what that label wants you to feel about it: pleased, if it flatters you; corrected, if it does not; and in either case, receptive. Write down your first emotional reaction to your report before you write anything analytical. That reaction, examined rather than suppressed, is legitimate evidence in Part 1, and Baer is the citation that licenses treating it that way.
- **Final essay:** Week 16 is the futures material for Part 2. A commitment only about this semester will not survive the conditions the ACRL report describes; a commitment that names a specific pressure from these documents (a defunded grant program, a vendor-integrated AI feature you did not choose, an accessibility standard now in force, the environmental cost of a system you use) and says what you will do when you meet it, will. The unresolved tension the essay requires is easiest to find here too: Baer and the two futures reports do not agree about whether adoption is a choice, and you need not pretend they do.
- **Threads back:** This closes the loop on the entire course. Block 1's philosophy, Block 2's privacy and access commitments, Block 3's equity framework, Block 4's AI scenarios, and Block 5's leadership, global, and preservation frames all have to appear in one document now. Reread your Block 1 philosophy statement this week before you write. The distance between it and what you would now write is one of the more interesting things you have to report.

## 📚 Going Deeper

Optional, and genuinely optional. These are also good places to find a final-essay angle.

- *In the Library with the Lead Pipe*, https://www.inthelibrarywiththeleadpipe.org/. Open, peer-reviewed, volunteer-run; much of the critical conversation Baer joins is happening here, and it is a realistic place to publish early in your career.

- Sano-Franchini, J., McIntyre, M., & Fernandes, M., *Refusing GenAI in Writing Studies: A Quickstart Guide*, https://refusinggenai.wordpress.com. The ten-premise document Baer draws on; short, open, directly usable. ---
