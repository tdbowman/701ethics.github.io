## 1. ALA (2019)

**Library Bill of Rights**

> American Library Association. (2019). Library Bill of Rights. American Library Association. (Adopted June 19, 1939; amended 1944, 1948, 1961, 1967, 1980, and January 29, 2019)

**Find it.** https://www.ala.org/advocacy/intfreedom/librarybill. Interpretations: https://www.ala.org/advocacy/intfreedom/librarybill/interpretations

**In the course project.** `ALALibrary Bill of Rights.pdf`

Seven articles. Article I requires resources for "the interest, information, and enlightenment of all people of the community" and forbids exclusion based on "the origin, background, or views of those contributing to their creation" (a provision about the author, not the content). Article II requires "all points of view on current and historical issues" and forbids removal for "partisan or doctrinal disapproval." Article III makes challenging censorship an affirmative duty. Article IV commits libraries to cooperate with others resisting abridgment of free expression. Article V protects users regardless of "origin, age, background, or views." Article VI requires equitable availability of meeting rooms and exhibit spaces "regardless of the beliefs or affiliations" of those requesting them. Article VII, added in 2019, establishes a right to privacy and confidentiality and obliges libraries to safeguard all library use data including personally identifiable information.

Two structural features matter more than any single article. The document is written almost entirely in terms of what libraries should *not* do, with Articles III and IV the exceptions imposing affirmative duties. And it concedes its own insufficiency: it acknowledges that "questions do arise concerning application of these principles to specific library practices" and directs readers to the Interpretations, which is where the profession does its actual reasoning and where you find the meeting room, programs and displays, and user-initiated exhibits documents Knox analyzes.

Note the internal tensions. Article II's "all points of view" is a collection-level aspiration, not an item-level rule; no library balances every book with its opposite. Article V's inclusion of "age" was reaffirmed in 1996 precisely because it is the article most contested by people who want minors treated differently. Article VII will collide, in Block 2, with the vendor and analytics infrastructure most libraries now run on.

:::{tip} Why it's here
Everything else this week is commentary on this text. Read it whole, and read the amendment history: a seven-article document amended seven times in eighty-six years tells you the profession's core commitments are revisable and have been revised under pressure.
:::

> "Libraries should provide materials and information presenting all points of view on current and historical issues. Materials should not be proscribed or removed because of partisan or doctrinal disapproval." (Library Bill of Rights, Article II)

## 2. Knox (2024)

**Embodiment, endorsement, and policy: Considerations for intellectual freedom in the library**

> Knox, E. J. M. (2024). Embodiment, endorsement, and policy: Considerations for intellectual freedom in the library. Information & Culture, 59(2), 109–124. https://doi.org/10.7560/IC59201

**Find it.** https://doi.org/10.7560/IC59201 · Project MUSE: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/931526 (Dominican has access; also in Perusall)

**In the course project.** `Knox2024EmbodimentEndorsementPolicy.pdf`

Knox's thesis is that policies are not descriptions of values but the embodiment of them, and that most conflicts labeled intellectual freedom disputes are really disputes about what a library's presence, sponsorship, or display of material *signifies*. She builds this on Bourdieu: libraries sit in the field of cultural production, where actors minimize economic power and maximize symbolic power. Bourdieu separates large-scale production (money, little prestige) from restricted-scale production (prestige, little money); libraries are anomalous because they collect both and shelve them by audience and subject rather than cultural rank. Hence her formulation that libraries "seem orderly at first glance" but are "actually quite messy when considered symbolically within a community." She adds machinery from Braman (policy is power), Brownson and colleagues ("big P" law versus "small p" organizational guidelines), and Nelson and Garcia (policy translates values into action, informs staff, assures equitable treatment, and provides a legal framework), and flags the *Intellectual Freedom Manual*'s six essential policies.

The case study is ALA's 2018 meeting room Interpretation: late edits inserting "hate speech" language, PLA having reviewed only an earlier draft, adoption without debate on June 26, the #NoHateALA backlash, rescission on August 16 by 140–4–2, and the January 2019 replacement stating that meeting rooms, if offered, are designated public forums from which libraries "may not exclude any group based on the subject matter to be discussed or the ideas for which the group advocates."

She then separates four conflated terms. **Endorsement** is open approval; libraries do not endorse collection contents, and policy should say so, because users do not understand how materials arrive and because acquisition runs through approval plans, consortial e-book deals, and databases the library does not curate title by title. **Sponsorship** means paying for or running a program; ALA holds this is not endorsement, though Knox concedes most people hear it that way. **Platforming** is giving a forum, and here she makes her most interesting concession: displays *are* platforming, because staff actively select them, so libraries should think harder about displays than acquisitions. **Viewpoint neutrality** is a First Amendment constraint on public forums, not a political stance; she quotes Chmara that content-based restrictions require a compelling interest and narrow tailoring while viewpoint restrictions "are impermissible in any form."

Her treatment of neutrality is even-handed. She documents that neutrality is not an ALA core value, tracing its unofficial adoption to Foskett's 1962 *Creed of a Librarian* while noting Foskett described the reference interview, not institutional posture. She grounds the profession's position in Mill's four arguments and Asheim's selector/censor distinction, then supplies the counterweight: libraries may amplify some speech, and ALA's hate speech FAQ endorses affirmative signage and programming. Her strongest practical argument against no-hate-speech clauses is strategic rather than legal: they "will not necessarily turn away Nazis" but could be turned against antiracist authors or a local Black Lives Matter chapter, since there is no agreed definition of hate speech. She closes by reasserting the individual: when libraries "lose sight of the individual and their information needs and focus solely on social groups," the library becomes indistinguishable from a commercial enterprise or a search engine.

:::{tip} Why it's here
Knox is the only Block 1 reading that theorizes *why* library conflicts run so hot relative to their apparent stakes, and the only one giving you working vocabulary for distinctions your critics will collapse. She is also a participant in the case she analyzes, which makes the article a lesson in how professional consensus is manufactured.
:::

> "Viewpoint neutrality does not mean that libraries are neutral. There are clear, codified core values in librarianship, as listed above, that stand on the side of human flourishing and autonomy." (Knox, 2024, p. 120)

## 3. Hepler & Horalek (2023)

**Introduction to library and information science**

> Hepler, R., & Horalek, D. (2023). Introduction to library and information science. College of Southern Idaho. (Chapters 11–14)

**Find it.** Ch. 11 "Intellectual Freedom": https://csi.pressbooks.pub/lis/chapter/intellectual-freedom/ · Ch. 12 "Digital Initiatives and Library 2.0": /chapter/digital-initiatives-and-library-2-0/ · Ch. 13 "Copyright": /chapter/copyright/ · Ch. 14 "Representation in the Library": /chapter/representation-in-the-library/

Chapter 11 is the direct companion to the Library Bill of Rights. It defines intellectual freedom as patrons' right to access materials and form independent judgments, walks through the seven articles and the nine ALA principles, and frames the practical difficulty as a tension among freedom, information democracy, and responsibility. It covers banned books and the challenge process, collection development pressure, children's internet access and filtering, and confidentiality of patron records. Where Knox is precise, this is introductory; read it for orientation and use Knox to sharpen it.

Chapter 13 is the most technically useful and sets up Week 3. U.S. protection attaches automatically on fixation in a tangible medium given minimal creativity. It walks through the four fair use factors of § 107 (purpose and character, including transformativeness; nature of the work, treating factual and unpublished works differently; amount and substantiality, including the "heart" of the work; and market effect) and stresses that fair use is case-by-case with "no formula." It then covers public domain, Creative Commons, open access, and OER. It also cites rule-of-thumb percentages for classroom copying; treat those as institutional guidelines, not law, because they are not in the statute.

Chapter 14 argues that representation alone is insufficient and must be paired with equity work, warning that "collections diversity will mean little if patrons who are members of minority groups do not feel welcome." Chapter 12 defines Library 2.0 as a user-centered, collaborative, technology-enabled service model and covers digitization, Omeka exhibits, makerspaces, social media, website analytics, and generative AI tools. It is the loosest fit for a censorship week; read it forward, as a preview of Blocks 2 and 4, and notice that its enthusiasm for collecting data on patron preferences sits uneasily against Article VII.

:::{tip} Why it's here
These chapters supply the practical scaffolding around Knox's theory: what a challenge process looks like, what fair use actually requires, how representation enters collection decisions. Chapters 13 and 14 do double duty, setting up Week 3's copyright Interpretation and Block 3's equity readings.
:::

## 4. Meehan et al. (2026)

**Facts & fiction: Stories stripped away by book bans**

> Meehan, K., Samson, M., Tamayo Latorre, Y., & Magnusson, T. (2026). Facts & fiction: Stories stripped away by book bans. PEN America.

**Find it.** https://pen.org/book-bans/ (press release: https://pen.org/press-release/banned-nonfiction-books-double-in-public-schools-erasing-authentic-stories-histories/). Companion: Baêta, S., Magnusson, T., Markham, M., Tamayo Latorre, Y., & Meehan, K. (2025). *The normalization of book banning: Banned in the USA, 2024–2025*. PEN America. https://pen.org/report/the-normalization-of-book-banning/

PEN counts differently from ALA, and the difference is instructive. PEN tracks *instances* of removal in public school libraries and classrooms, drawn from district records, news reports, and public information requests, rather than challenges reported across all library types. For 2024–2025, *The Normalization of Book Banning* documented 6,870 instances affecting 3,752 unique titles across 87 districts in 23 states, with 2,589 creative professionals affected and 590 bans in Department of Defense Education Activity schools; Florida alone accounted for 2,304. Cumulatively since July 2021 the count stands at 22,810 cases across 45 states and 451 districts. The title is the argument: bans have become procedurally routine rather than exceptional.

*Facts & Fiction* re-cuts the same year by genre and content and finds nonfiction is now a major target: 3,743 unique titles and 6,780 instances across 23 states, of which 29% were nonfiction and 13% fell in an educational or informational genre, with 52% of banned nonfiction titles (1,102) involving activism themes. Across all unique banned titles, 39% feature LGBTQ+ characters or people, 19% transgender characters or people, 44% Black characters or other characters of color, and 10% disabled or neurodivergent characters or people; 34% (1,270) include consensual sexual experiences and 19% (724) include sexual violence.

Where you should push back: PEN's definition of a ban includes restricted access and removals pending review, broader than permanent withdrawal, and that is where methodological critics focus. Read the methodology section, not the press release. The defensible claim, and the one that matters for the scenario, is not a headline number but the compositional finding that removed material is not randomly distributed. Note also that ALA's 4,235 unique titles for calendar 2025 and PEN's 3,743 for the 2024–25 school year are not comparable; anyone adding them or treating them as corroborating measures of the same thing is misusing both.

:::{tip} Why it's here
ALA tells you who is challenging; PEN tells you what is removed and in what proportions. Together they let you make an evidentiary rather than a values argument, which is the argument that survives a hostile board meeting. Their methodological differences also give you a live case in information policy analysis (Objective O4).
:::
