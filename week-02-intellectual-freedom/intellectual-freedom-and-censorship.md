# Week 2: Intellectual Freedom & Censorship 📚

**Block 1: Foundations & Intellectual Freedom | August 31 – September 6, 2026**

---

*Everyone agrees censorship is wrong. Almost no one agrees on what counts as censorship.*

Week 1 asked what the profession is. This week asks what it promises, and shows that the promise is harder to keep than it sounds. The Library Bill of Rights is seven sentences. The Interpretations attached to it run to dozens of documents, because seven sentences cannot decide real cases. Emily Knox studies exactly that gap; the PEN America data shows the scale at which the gap is now being exploited.

The problem is the difference between a value and a policy. Knox's answer is the week's thesis: policies are the **embodiment** of an institution's values, and it is policies, not collections or programs, that put values into action. That has a hard consequence. A library with no written meeting room policy has not committed to anything, whatever its staff believe. It has a second, less comfortable one: a policy that genuinely embodies intellectual freedom will sometimes produce outcomes the staff who wrote it find intolerable. Knox's case study is exactly that situation, and you should read her account of the 2018 ALA meeting room controversy, including the April Hathcock blog post she quotes, as evidence that consensus documents fail precisely where professional values genuinely conflict.

### 🎯 What You'll Learn

- Draw the line between selection and censorship, and between a challenge and a ban
- Work through Knox on embodiment, endorsement, and what a policy actually does when it is invoked
- Read the Library Bill of Rights as the operative text it is, Article VII included
- Ask what PEN America's counts measure, and what they cannot

### 🧠 Big Questions to Consider

- Knox says neutrality is not a core value of librarianship and barely appears in ALA policy. If so, what is the profession committed to when it refuses to remove a book it finds repugnant?
- Asheim distinguishes the selector, who looks for reasons to keep a book, from the censor, who looks for reasons to reject it. Is that about the act, the intent, or the outcome, and does it survive a shrinking materials budget?
- If including a book is not an endorsement but a display *is* a platform, where does the line fall, and can you write a policy that draws it?
