## 🗝️ Key Concepts

| Term | What it means |
|------|---------------|
| **Embodiment of values in policy** | Knox's central claim: policies do not merely reflect institutional values, they are the concrete form those values take. A value with no policy is not operative. |
| **Big P / small p policy** | Formal law and regulation versus organizational guidelines and internal norms. Libraries mostly govern by small p but are increasingly constrained by big P. |
| **Field of cultural production** | Bourdieu's term, applied by Knox: the space where symbolic capital outranks economic capital. Libraries are anomalous because they collect both large-scale and restricted-scale production. |
| **Endorsement vs. sponsorship vs. platforming** | Inclusion is not approval; running a program is sponsorship, which ALA holds is also not endorsement; selecting material for a display is platforming, which Knox concedes carries institutional weight. |
| **Viewpoint neutrality** | A legal constraint on designated public forums: a library that opens meeting rooms may not exclude groups for the ideas they advocate. Distinct from political neutrality. |
| **Content-based vs. viewpoint-based restriction** | Chmara, quoted by Knox: content restrictions in an open forum survive only with a compelling interest and narrow tailoring; viewpoint restrictions "are impermissible in any form." |
| **Selection vs. censorship** | Asheim's 1953 formulation: the selector looks for reasons to keep a work, the censor for reasons to reject it. A claim about disposition, not the physical act. |
| **Designated public forum** | The status a library creates by opening meeting rooms; the 2019 Interpretation notes libraries need not offer them but incur forum obligations if they do. |
| **Challenge vs. ban** | ALA counts documented attempts to redact, restrict, relocate, or remove; PEN counts instances of removal or restricted access in schools. Different denominators, not additive. |

All terms from all sixteen weeks are collected in the [Glossary](../glossary.md).

## 💬 Annotation & Discussion Prompts

These are written for **Perusall**. Each one points at something specific and each one is arguable, which is the point: annotation credit comes from advancing a conversation, not from summarizing a paragraph.

1. Annotate Knox's claim that "it is the policies themselves that put values into action and not the materials, programs, displays, or exhibits" (p. 120). If a library has a superb collection and no written reconsideration policy, has it committed to intellectual freedom? Defend your answer against the obvious objection.
2. Read Knox on the 2018 meeting room revision with the Hathcock quotation she reproduces. Was Council's August 2018 rescission a defense of process or a capitulation to pressure? Make the strongest case for the reading you do *not* hold before stating yours.
3. Knox argues displays are platforming though collections are not. Write one sentence of display policy that would govern a Pride display and a display by a controversial conservative author under the same rule, then say honestly whether you would apply it evenly.
4. Annotate Article II against PEN's finding that removals disproportionately target LGBTQ+ and BIPOC narratives. Does Article II obligate a library to acquire material it believes factually false, and does the answer change for health and history nonfiction?
5. Knox reports the strategic argument against no-hate-speech clauses: they will not exclude the people you want excluded and can be turned against antiracist material. Is that persuasive on its merits, or a rationalization of professional discomfort with taking sides?
6. ALA counts 4,235 unique titles challenged in calendar 2025; PEN counts 3,743 banned in the 2024–25 school year. Explain to a skeptical trustee, in plain language, why these cannot be added, averaged, or used to check each other.
7. Under a shrinking budget you decline to buy a poorly written book a community group wants. By Asheim's test, are you a selector or a censor? Argue it.

## 🔗 Connections

- **Scenario:** This is the operational core of "The Censorship Crisis." The **Challenge** and **Choices** stages will hand you a reconsideration request, community pressure, and a board with its own view. Knox supplies the sentences you need; the Library Bill of Rights supplies authority; ALA and PEN supply the read on whether you face one patron or one campaign. Before you play, know which of the six essential policies your fictional library would need in place.
- **Final essay:** Knox's endorsement/platforming distinction maps onto algorithmic recommendation. A collection includes; a recommender platforms. A discovery system that surfaces some titles and buries others makes display decisions at scale with no policy, no disclaimer, and no reconsideration process. That is a strong Part 1 argument and a genuine Block 1 to Block 4 bridge for the required cross-block citation.
- **Threads forward:** Week 3 turns these aspirations into obligations and exposes a real conflict, since the IFLA code embraces the word "neutrality" Knox says American librarianship never officially adopted. Block 2 develops Article VII; Block 3 develops the equity critique Knox names as in tension with intellectual freedom absolutism.

Optional, and genuinely optional. These are also good places to find a final-essay angle.

- ALA, "Meeting Rooms: An Interpretation of the Library Bill of Rights" (January 29, 2019): https://www.ala.org/advocacy/intfreedom/librarybill/interpretations/meetingrooms — The document Knox's working group produced; read it after her account of how it was made.
- Asheim, L. (1953). Not censorship but selection. *Wilson Library Bulletin, 28*(1), 63–67. — Seventy years old, still the most cited distinction in the field, readable in fifteen minutes.

- Garnar, M., & Magi, T. (Eds.). (2021). *Intellectual freedom manual* (10th ed.). American Library Association. — Source of Knox's six essential policies, and the handbook you will actually use on the job. ---
