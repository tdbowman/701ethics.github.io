## 1. Robinson et al. (2020)

**Digital inequalities 3.0: Emergent inequalities in the information age**

> Robinson, L., Schulz, J., Dunn, H. S., Casilli, A. A., Tubaro, P., Carvath, R., Chen, W., Wiest, J. B., Dodel, M., Stern, M. J., Ball, C., Huang, K.-T., Blank, G., Ragnedda, M., Ono, H., Hogan, B., Mesch, G. S., Cotten, S. R., Kretchmer, S. B., Hale, T. M., Drabowicz, T., Yan, P., Wellman, B., Harper, M.-G., Quan-Haase, A., & Khilnani, A. (2020). Digital inequalities 3.0: Emergent inequalities in the information age. First Monday, 25(7). https://doi.org/10.5210/fm.v25i7.10844

**Find it.** https://doi.org/10.5210/fm.v25i7.10844 (open access)

This is the second of a two-article series marking the twenty-fifth anniversary of the NTIA's 1995 coinage of "digital divide." The first, *Digital inequalities 2.0: Legacy inequalities in the information age*, introduced the organizing metaphor: the **digital inequality stack**, "the complex layers that must all work together to produce digital inclusion," where legacy axes (class, gender, sexuality, race and ethnicity, aging, disability, healthcare, education) persist. *3.0* asks what is new.

The article proceeds through eleven substantive sections, each a short synthesis by different members of the author collective: accessibility as a human right; the platform economy and digital labor; automation; big data and algorithms; digital intersections with criminal justice and security; cybersafety; civic engagement and mobility; gaming; well-being and the life course; assistive technologies; and a concluding implications section. The breadth is the method. The claim is that digital inequality is no longer a topic within media studies but a dimension of nearly every other inequality.

Several sections are directly usable here. On **access as a right**, the authors cite affordability as the primary barrier for the half of the world unable to connect, with 1GB of data averaging roughly seven percent of monthly salary across Africa and as much as twenty percent in some countries. On the **platform economy**, gig platforms widen participation while reinforcing inequality through low pay and absent protections: "[p]latforms produce a shift in the balance of power between capital and labor." On **big data and algorithms**, algorithmic systems make consequential decisions about credit, hiring, healthcare, insurance, and sentencing while embedding biases their subjects cannot inspect. On **criminal justice and security**, surveillance technologies land disproportionately on already-disadvantaged communities. On **cybersafety**, the skills to protect oneself are themselves unequally distributed, which makes privacy a competency gap and not only a right. The conclusion argues that if "big tech and the digital oligarchy" continue to profit from and de facto regulate digital life, the stack will keep growing "to the detriment of the many," and that digital resources should therefore be treated as civil and human rights to be promoted and cultivated.

:::{tip} Why it's here
It replaces the 2015 article because that article's frame (access, usage, skills, self-perceptions) no longer covers the terrain. *3.0* connects this week to Weeks 4 and 5 rather than running parallel: its algorithms, cybersafety, and criminal-justice sections are digital-divide arguments *and* privacy arguments, which is the synthesis the block is building toward. The superseded 2015 article (Robinson, Cotten, Ono, Quan-Haase, Mesch, Chen, Schulz, Hale, & Stern, *Information, Communication & Society, 18*(5), 569–582, https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2015.1012532) remains in the project and is worth reading for its life-course, gender, race, and eHealth sections, which *3.0* compresses.
:::

> "While many users are competent prosumers who nimbly work within different layers of the stack, very few individuals are 'full stack engineers' able to create or recreate digital devices, networks[.]" (Abstract)

## 2. Pew Research Center (2026)

**What we know about internet use, smartphone ownership and digital divides in the U.S**

> Pew Research Center. (2026, January 8). What we know about internet use, smartphone ownership and digital divides in the U.S. (C. McClain & W. Bishop). Pew Research Center.

**Find it.** https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/01/08/internet-use-smartphone-ownership-digital-divides-in-u-s/. See also the continuously updated fact sheets at https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/internet-broadband/ and https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/mobile/

This reading gives you data rather than an argument, and it is the empirical floor under everything else this week. The 2025 NPORS figures: **96%** of US adults use the internet; **91%** own a smartphone (98% own a cellphone of some kind); **78%** subscribe to home broadband; **16%** are smartphone dependent, owning a smartphone but having no home broadband subscription.

The distributions are where the argument lives. On broadband and income, Pew reports that in households making under $30,000 a year, "54% do, compared with 94% of those in the highest-income households – a 40 percentage point gap." On smartphones and age: "97% of adults under 50 and 90% of those ages 50 to 64 have a smartphone. Those 65 and older are least likely to own one, but 78% of them still do." On smartphone dependence and income: "About a third of those in households earning less than $30,000 annually are smartphone dependent, compared with 4% of those earning $100,000 or more." Pew also reports that Black and Hispanic adults are less likely than White or Asian adults to subscribe to broadband, and that urban and rural adults subscribe at similar rates while both trail suburban adults.

Two cautions that matter for citation. First, Pew shifted from telephone to a multimode (web and mail) address-based protocol after 2021 and warns explicitly against reading pre- and post-2021 figures as a clean trend line. Second, "smartphone dependent" is a constructed category, the intersection of two survey answers, not a self-report; when you use it, say what it means. For comparison, the 2024 report in the project (*Americans' Use of Mobile Technology and Home Broadband*, Gelles-Watnick, January 31, 2024, fielded May 19 – September 5, 2023, n = 5,733) reported 95% internet use, 90% smartphone ownership, 80% home broadband, 15% smartphone dependent, with broadband at 57% under $30,000 versus 95% over $100,000, and 73% rural versus 86% suburban. Headline home broadband is *down* two points between surveys while internet use and smartphone ownership are up one each; do not narrate that as a decline without checking margins of error (±1.8 points in 2023, ±1.9 in 2025).

:::{tip} Why it's here
Arguments about digital equity collapse without numbers, and being precise about which survey, which year, and which constructed category you are quoting is itself a professional competency. The Digital Divide Community Assessment depends on your doing this correctly.
:::

> "In this lowest-income group, 54% do, compared with 94% of those in the highest-income households – a 40 percentage point gap." (Section on home broadband)

## 3. Grimes & Porter (2024)

**Closing the digital divide through digital equity: The role of libraries and librarians**

> Grimes, N. D., & Porter, W. (2024). Closing the digital divide through digital equity: The role of libraries and librarians. Public Library Quarterly, 43(3), 307–338. https://doi.org/10.1080/01616846.2023.2251348

**Find it.** https://doi.org/10.1080/01616846.2023.2251348 (open access, published under license by Taylor & Francis)

**In the course project.** `Grimes.2024.ClosingDigitalDivide.pdf`

Grimes and Porter write as successors to two heavily cited *PLQ* articles, Kinney (2010) and Jaeger et al. (2012), and their thesis is practical: libraries should stop treating digital equity as outreach and write it into their strategic plans as core mission.

The article moves through five arguments. A **history**: 21% of US public libraries had an internet connection in 1994; a decade later more than 99% did, and by Bertot et al.'s 2011 survey nearly 65% of public libraries reported being the only free public-access computing and internet center in their community. A **definitional move** borrowed from NDIA, distinguishing the digital *divide* (the disparity) from digital *equity* (the condition) and digital *inclusion* (the activities). A **policy inventory**: the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law's $65 billion broadband investment, the $42.45 billion BEAD program allocated to all fifty states, DC, and five territories in June 2023, the $930 million middle-mile program, nearly $1 billion for Tribal lands, and the $2.75 billion Digital Equity Act funding state digital equity plans from 2021 to 2026. A **firsthand account**: co-author Porter served as the New Jersey Library Association's representative on the state's Broadband Access Study Commission, and reports a commission member arguing that libraries were not frequented by low-income residents and were therefore unsuitable outreach sites. He proposes libraries as a "failover mechanism" (the connection that survives eviction, moving, and inability to pay a subsidized bill), argues for gigabit fiber as a minimum standard for every library in the state, and offers a five-layer "protocol stack" for access: adequate broadband on site, hardware, reliable user access to that hardware, exposure to needed software, and training in both.

Most usable is the **strategy section**, which adopts Barbakoff's four digital-inclusion approaches: **digital navigators** (a reference-interview model for technology need, pioneered by Salt Lake City Public Library with NDIA, IMLS, and the Urban Libraries Council); **community networks** (cooperatively owned neighborhood infrastructure, exemplified by the Detroit Community Technology Project's Equitable Internet Initiative and its digital stewards); **facilitated access** (hybrid programming, and library-hosted telehealth where the library supplies bandwidth, a semi-private room, and proximity to transit); and **community organizing and advocacy**. They close by calling for public-private partnerships, documenting PLA's arrangements with AT&T, Microsoft's Airband Initiative, and Capital One.

A caution for your annotations: this is an advocacy article that argues by citation accumulation rather than original evidence. Several statistics are quoted at second and third hand, and at least one block quotation is attributed to the wrong source in the published text. Read it for its framework and policy map; verify its numbers against the primary source before reusing them.

:::{tip} Why it's here
It is the only assigned reading that answers "so what does a library actually do?" with named, funded, replicable programs. You also get the vocabulary that appears in state and federal grant language: equity plan, digital navigator, community anchor institution, failover.
:::

> "It is evident that public libraries serve as a bridge to connect people to the worldwide connected digital community in the 21st century." (Section: A brief history of libraries and the digital divide, p. 310)

## 4. NDIA (n.d.)

**Definitions: The words behind our work**

> National Digital Inclusion Alliance. (n.d.). Definitions: The words behind our work. https://www.digitalinclusion.org/definitions/

**Find it.** https://www.digitalinclusion.org/definitions/. See also the *Digital Navigator Model* (https://www.digitalinclusion.org/digital-navigator-model/)

NDIA is the US practitioner coalition for digital inclusion, and its definitions page is why the field's vocabulary is consistent across ALA, NTIA, and state broadband offices. The federal Digital Equity Act adopted this framing. Three definitions do the work. **Digital inclusion** is "[a]ctivities necessary to ensure that all individuals and communities, including the most disadvantaged, have access to and use of Information and Communication Technologies (ICTs)," with five required elements: affordable robust broadband, internet-enabled devices that meet the user's needs, digital literacy training, quality technical support, and applications and content designed to enable and encourage self-sufficiency, participation, and collaboration. **Digital equity** is "[a] condition in which all individuals and communities have the information technology capacity needed for full participation in our society, democracy and economy." **Digital literacy** NDIA takes from ALA: "[t]he ability to use information and communication technologies to find, evaluate, create, and communicate information, requiring both cognitive and technical skills."

The structure of the distinction is the argument. Inclusion is *activity*, what you do. Equity is *condition*, the state you are trying to reach. Literacy is *capability*, what a person can do. Grant reviewers know the difference: a proposal promising equity while describing only device lending has confused a condition with an activity. Note also that NDIA's five elements make devices, support, and relevant content co-equal with connectivity, which is why "we have broadband now" is not an answer to this week's question.

:::{tip} Why it's here
Short, authoritative, and the definitional spine of the Digital Divide Community Assessment. It also disciplines the loose usage in the other readings: Grimes and Porter cite NDIA and then use "divide," "equity," and "inclusion" somewhat interchangeably, a good live example of why definitions matter.
:::

> "Digital equity is a condition in which all individuals and communities have the information technology capacity needed for full participation in our society, democracy and economy." (Definitions: Digital Equity)
