# Week 6: Digital Divide & Technology Access 📶

**Block 2: Privacy, Data Ethics & Digital Equity | September 28 – October 4, 2026**

---

*The divide is not a line between online and offline. It is a stack, and libraries sit partway up it.*

The Data Ethics Simulation is due at the end of this week, Sunday, October 4, and the sequencing is deliberate. You have spent two weeks learning why libraries restrict data collection. This week asks what those restrictions mean for people who have no other computer — and whether the profession's privacy commitments and its access commitments are in tension, aligned, or both depending on who you are.

Start with the numbers, because they discipline the argument. Pew's most recent tracking, fielded February 5 to June 18, 2025 and reported in January 2026, finds 96% of US adults using the internet, 91% owning a smartphone, and 78% subscribing to home broadband. That last figure is the one to sit with: nearly one in four American adults has no broadband at home, and 16% are "smartphone dependent," owning a smartphone but no home subscription. The gaps are not evenly distributed — broadband runs 54% in households under $30,000 against 94% in households over $100,000, a forty-point gap, and roughly a third of the lowest-income group is smartphone dependent against 4% of the highest.

### 🎯 What You'll Learn

- Move from access to adoption to the full digital inequality stack
- Read Pew's 2026 figures precisely, including what smartphone dependence actually means
- Distinguish digital equity, digital inclusion, and digital literacy using NDIA's definitions
- Assess honestly whether your library is a solution or a failover mechanism

### 🧠 Big Questions to Consider

- If 78% home broadband is the highest figure ever recorded and the income gap is still forty points, what would count as progress, and who decides?
- What can a single public library actually change about an inequality produced by infrastructure investment, pricing, and labor markets — and what does it distort by claiming it can?
- When privacy protection makes a service harder to use for the patrons who most depend on it, whose interest should govern, and what would you build to avoid having to choose?
