## 🗝️ Key Concepts

| Term | What it means |
|------|---------------|
| **Digital inequality stack** | Robinson et al.'s layered model: inclusion requires access, use, consumption, literacies, skills, and production all working together; a gap in any layer produces exclusion regardless of the others. |
| **Prosumers vs. full stack engineers** | The emergent production divide: most competent users work *within* the layers; very few can create or recreate devices, networks, and platforms, and that asymmetry concentrates power in technology creators. |
| **Adoption vs. access** | Grimes and Porter's distinction from the New Jersey commission's work: availability of a broadband line is not the same as a household taking it up, and subsidy programs address the second only partially because of mobility, eviction, and payment interruption. |
| **Smartphone dependence** | Pew's constructed category for adults owning a smartphone but no home broadband: 16% of US adults in 2025, about a third of households under $30,000 against 4% over $100,000. |
| **Digital equity** | NDIA: a *condition* in which all individuals and communities have the IT capacity needed for full participation in society, democracy, and economy. |
| **Digital inclusion** | NDIA: the *activities* required to reach that condition, comprising affordable broadband, adequate devices, literacy training, technical support, and enabling content. |
| **Digital navigator** | A trusted guide who assists community members with internet adoption and device use; operationally, a reference interview about technology need that addresses access, device, and skill in one encounter. |
| **Community anchor institution / failover mechanism** | Porter's framing of the library as the connection that persists regardless of housing status or ability to pay a subsidized bill; the argument for gigabit fiber as a library minimum. |
| **Facilitated access** | Barbakoff's term, via Grimes and Porter, for lowering the barrier to complex technology rather than merely providing it: hybrid programming, staff-mediated sessions, library-hosted telehealth space. |
| **Digital redlining** | The pattern by which infrastructure investment, pricing, and service tiers track existing residential segregation, producing geographically uneven connectivity no individual skill can overcome. (Not a term in the assigned readings; cite it to the literature, not to Robinson or Grimes.) |

All terms from all sixteen weeks are collected in the [Glossary](../glossary.md).

## 💬 Annotation & Discussion Prompts

These are written for **Perusall**. Each one points at something specific and each one is arguable, which is the point: annotation credit comes from advancing a conversation, not from summarizing a paragraph.

1. Pew reports 78% home broadband, the profession's usual framing is "the digital divide persists," and Robinson et al. argue the access frame is outdated. Annotate the passage in *3.0* where they make that case. Is 22% without broadband a legacy problem or an emergent one, and does the answer change what a library should build?
2. Porter reports a state commission member asserting that libraries are not used by low-income residents. Mark that passage. What evidence from this week would you have put in front of that commission, and which of it would have been persuasive rather than merely correct?
3. Robinson et al. argue the emergent divide separates those who can create digital systems from those who can only use them. Grimes and Porter's remedies mostly produce better users. Mismatch between diagnosis and treatment, or the only intervention available at library scale?
4. NDIA lists five elements of digital inclusion, only one of which is connectivity. Score a library you know against all five. Which element is it weakest on, and is that a funding problem or a design problem?
5. Weeks 4 and 5 argued for data minimization and opt-out defaults; Chin et al.'s participants described patrons who need staff to enter their information for them. Annotate the passage where these commitments collide, and propose a service design that does not force the trade.
6. Grimes and Porter call for partnerships with Google, Microsoft, AT&T, and Capital One, and document existing PLA arrangements with three of them. Robinson et al. warn about "big tech and the digital oligarchy" de facto regulating digital life. Can a library accept the first while believing the second?
7. Pew shifted survey mode in 2021 and warns against reading a clean trend across the break. Grimes and Porter cite 2019 and 2021 Pew figures alongside 2016 ones as if they were a series. Find a place where that matters to their argument.

## 🔗 Connections

- **Scenario:** This week makes the simulation's community-demands branch legible. When residents ask for personalization, saved search history, self-service holds, or staff help entering their own data, the population you are serving is the one Pew describes: smartphone dependent, no home broadband, low margin for a breach. Robinson et al.'s cybersafety section is why the equity answer is not simply "give them what they ask for": the skills to protect oneself are unequally distributed, so a library that offloads privacy decisions onto patrons distributes risk regressively. NDIA's five elements are a checklist for the simulation's service-design choices, and Grimes and Porter's digital-navigator model is the concrete alternative to a false choice between privacy and help. Due Sunday, October 4; 75/100 to pass, unlimited retries. Use the first attempt diagnostically.
- **Final essay:** "Arguing with the Algorithm" requires four readings from two blocks including Block 4. Block 2 is the natural second block, and this week supplies the distributive argument the essay usually lacks: algorithmic systems do not harm everyone equally, and Robinson et al.'s big-data-and-algorithms and criminal-justice sections give you cited grounds for saying so. If your Part 2 commitments are about privacy alone, this week is what stops them from being commitments that protect only people like you.
- **Threads forward:** Block 3 takes the equity argument from infrastructure into collections, description, and social-justice practice, where the question becomes who is represented rather than who is connected. Block 4 returns to Robinson et al.'s algorithms section with far more specificity. Block 5 asks for an actionable professional position, and "digital equity" is the phrase most likely to appear in the job description you eventually answer.

## 📚 Going Deeper

Optional, and genuinely optional. These are also good places to find a final-essay angle.

- Robinson, L., et al. (2020). Digital inequalities 2.0: Legacy inequalities in the information age. *First Monday, 25*(7). https://doi.org/10.5210/fm.v25i7.10842. The companion article, where the stack is defined and the legacy axes surveyed.
- **NDIA Digital Navigator Model and Toolkit** (digitalinclusion.org) is the operational documentation behind the model Grimes and Porter recommend; developed with the Urban Libraries Council and IMLS.
- **PLA DigitalLearn** (digitallearn.org) and Tools and Resources for Trainers (training.digitallearn.org): free tutorials at a fourth-grade reading level in English and Spanish, plus 80+ ready-made training kits.
- **Pew Research Center internet and mobile fact sheets** (pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/) are continuously updated, and the right citation target when you need a current number rather than a report's snapshot.
- Robinson, L., Cotten, S. R., Ono, H., Quan-Haase, A., Mesch, G., Chen, W., Schulz, J., Hale, T. M., & Stern, M. J. (2015). Digital inequalities and why they matter. *Information, Communication & Society, 18*(5), 569–582. https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2015.1012532. The superseded article, in the project; still the best short treatment of digital inequality across the life course and in eHealth.
