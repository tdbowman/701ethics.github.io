## 1. ALA (2025)

**Privacy: An interpretation of the Library Bill of Rights**

> American Library Association. (2025). Privacy: An interpretation of the Library Bill of Rights. Adopted June 19, 2002; amended July 1, 2014, June 24, 2019, and June 29, 2025.

**Find it.** https://www.ala.org/advocacy/intfreedom/librarybill/interpretations/privacy

**In the course project.** `ALAPrivacy_ An Interpretation of the Library Bill of Rights _ ALA.pdf`

The interpretation grounds privacy in a chilling-effect argument rather than a proprietary one: "When users recognize or fear that their privacy is at risk, they lose the freedom to explore ideas." Privacy is instrumental to intellectual freedom, and the document builds a legal scaffold under that claim — First Amendment cases on the right to receive information (*Pico*, *Kreimer*, *Reno*), privacy cases (*NAACP v. Alabama*, *Griswold*, *Katz*, *Stanley*), and the note that forty-eight states protect library-record confidentiality by statute while attorneys general in the other two have issued supporting opinions. It then distinguishes what students routinely conflate: *privacy* is the user's right to seek information without the subject of that interest being scrutinized; *confidentiality* is the library's responsibility once it holds PII — reference questions and interviews, circulation records, digital interactions, and records of use of resources, services, programs, or facilities.

The operational sections are where the 2025 revision does its work. Under **Rights of Library Users**, libraries may not limit privacy rights on the basis of age, ability, housing status, immigration status, criminal-justice involvement, religion, ethnicity, sexual orientation, or gender identity unless required by law, and minors hold the same rights as adults; nonessential collection must be off by default, opt-in, and reversible. Under **Data Protection**, the document imports the NISO Consensus Principles wholesale as its security baseline (encryption at rest and in motion, prompt patching, access control, security training, written breach procedures, configuration auditing) and adopts three GDPR-derived principles by name: purpose limitation, storage limitation, data minimization. Under **Library Vendors**, agreements must retain library ownership of user data, permit audits of vendor collection and access, and bind vendors to public NISO-conformant privacy policies. Under **Law Enforcement**, records are released only on properly executed court orders or legal process, with trained staff following documented procedures.

:::{tip} Why it's here
This is the profession's own answer sheet, placed first so everything after reads as a stress test. It is also the document the Data Ethics Simulation scores you against: the law-enforcement, vendor-negotiation, and default-settings branches map almost one-to-one onto its four operational sections.
:::

> "Any data collected for analysis should be anonymous or aggregated, it should never be linked to personal information. Biometric technologies, like facial recognition, do not align with the library's mission of facilitating access without unjust surveillance." (Section: Data Protection)

## 2. ALA Privacy Subcommittee (2019)

**Privacy and confidentiality Q&A**

> American Library Association, Intellectual Freedom Committee, Privacy Subcommittee. (2019). Privacy and confidentiality Q&A. Approved April 14, 2005; amended June 26 and October 30, 2006, January 23, 2012, July 1, 2014, and July 29, 2019.

**Find it.** https://www.ala.org/advocacy/intfreedom/privacyconfidentialityqa

**In the course project.** `ALAPrivacy and Confidentiality QA _ ALA.pdf`

The Q&A is the interpretation's implementation manual, in four parts: basic concepts, protection of privacy and library records, security concerns, and minors' privacy rights. Its value is that it works at the grain of actual decisions. Question 3 separates explicit consent from opt-out and states that library online services should not default to opt-out. Question 9 defines the **privacy audit** as an ongoing process rather than a one-time solution — policy review, categorization of data by sensitivity, assessment of necessity for each data type, and mapping of data flows for access, storage, and disposal.

Question 11 is the one to sit with: the closest thing the profession has to an official statement on library analytics. It concedes that "libraries depend on data for decision making," then states that CRMs and analytics tools "by default … collect a wide range of personal data from library users, creating a user profile not unlike the profiles created by major companies, such as Amazon and Facebook," and that "user profiling and other methods to track individual users directly conflict with the library user's right to privacy." Its four risk-reduction moves are worth memorizing: resist collecting data "just in case"; avoid high-risk data (citizenship and immigration status are the named examples); negotiate vendor collection defaults contractually; and aggregate, truncate, or obfuscate — while conceding that this alone "will not [protect] user privacy, and in some cases will not prevent individuals from using a changed data set from re-identifying a library user."

The security and minors sections supply specifics you will need for the simulation: sniffer software and spyware may violate the Electronic Communications Privacy Act and generate records subject to state confidentiality statutes; a library must not "serve as a surveillance outpost for law enforcement"; surveillance images may be disclosed voluntarily only where they do not reveal use of specific materials, and that call belongs to the director with counsel; and FERPA *permits* but does not *require* disclosures that state statutes and professional ethics would otherwise forbid.

:::{tip} Why it's here
The interpretation states principles; the Q&A shows where principles break into cases. It is also the only assigned text that treats the library worker as a person with divided loyalties — employee, user, steward of other people's records — which is the position the simulation puts you in.
:::

> "A library cannot be responsible for someone being seen or recognized in a library but should take steps to protect user privacy whenever possible. That is, in a library, a user's face may be recognized, but that does not mean that the subject of the user's interest must also be known." (Section I, Question 4)

## 3. Gstrein & Beaulieu (2022)

**How to protect privacy in a datafied society**

> Gstrein, O. J., & Beaulieu, A. (2022). How to protect privacy in a datafied society? A presentation of multiple legal and conceptual approaches. Philosophy & Technology, 35(1), Article 3. https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-022-00497-4

**Find it.** https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-022-00497-4 (open access, CC BY 4.0)

**In the course project.** `Gstrein.2022.ProtectPrivacy.pdf`

Gstrein and Beaulieu argue that "privacy" names not one protectable thing but a family of incompatible traditions, and that the incompatibilities matter because data now circulates across the jurisdictions where those traditions developed. Section 2 defines **datafication** through Beaulieu and Leonelli's four-layer model — the *community* of actors who handle data, the *forms of care* (meaning, valuation, law, maintenance), the *capacities* (skills, storage, statistical and algorithmic technique), and the *data*. The point is that you cannot regulate datafication by intervening in one technology or sector, because the layers feed back into each other.

Section 3 walks four legal approaches and three emerging ones. The legal set: the US Fourth Amendment tradition (privacy as the right to be left alone, defined by "reasonable expectation," historically about premises, now stretched by *Katz*, *Jones*, and *Carpenter*); the Fair Information Practice Principles, whose operative pair is **notice and choice**; the European "omnibus" **data protection** regime, principle-based and technology-neutral, running from the early-1970s Hesse and Swedish laws through Convention 108 to the GDPR; the South American **habeas data** doctrine, born of dictatorship and the search for the *desaparecidos*; and the German right to **informational self-determination**, derived by the Federal Constitutional Court in the 1983 census judgment from human dignity and free development of personality. The emerging set: **differential privacy** (calibrated noise, governed by accuracy, noise, and a "privacy budget"); **contextual integrity** (Nissenbaum's theory that privacy is appropriate information flow, specified by subject, sender, recipient, information type, transmission principle); and **group privacy**, which responds to harms like predictive policing that attach to categories rather than identifiable individuals.

Their critical claims are sharp. Notice and choice fails because notices are long and choice is artificial when refusal costs employment, education, or health care. Data protection's individual focus misses collective harm. Contextual integrity, adopted uncritically, may let powerful actors define "appropriate" flows and export a lowest-common-denominator standard. Group privacy is conceptually attractive and operationally unclear — groups often do not know they are groups. The conclusion is deliberately anti-solutionist: rather than one global answer, identify "synergies and stumbling blocks" between approaches.

:::{tip} Why it's here
This keeps the week from becoming a compliance exercise. It supplies vocabulary — datafication, contextual integrity, group privacy, technological gentrification, shadow profiles — you will need in Block 4 when the object of critique is an algorithm rather than a circulation record, and it makes the strongest available case that the ALA's individual-rights framing is not sufficient on its own.
:::

> "[M]any insights about individuals rely on assumptions about populations and groups, whereas most privacy and data protection regimes focus on atomistic agents and their individual rights, often labelling them as autonomous individuals." (Section 1, "Living in the digital age," p. 3)

## 4. Ocks & Salubi (2024)

**Privacy paradox in Industry 4.0: A review of library information services and data protection**

> Ocks, Y., & Salubi, O. G. (2024). Privacy paradox in Industry 4.0: A review of library information services and data protection. South African Journal of Information Management, 26(1), Article a1845. https://doi.org/10.4102/sajim.v26i1.1845

**Find it.** https://doi.org/10.4102/sajim.v26i1.1845 (open access)

**In the course project.** `Ocks.2024.PrivacyParadox.pdf`

Ocks and Salubi run a PRISMA-guided systematic review of peer-reviewed English-language literature from 2013 to 2023 on Fourth Industrial Revolution technologies in libraries, screening 7,330 records down to 27 included articles, and read the results against South Africa's Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA), whose main provisions commenced 1 July 2020. The "privacy paradox" is the gap between what patrons say they want and what they do — and institutionally, between what libraries profess and what their systems collect.

The empirical material is the useful part. From Avuglah et al.'s Ghanaian study: 91.7% of student respondents were very or somewhat concerned about privacy online; 83% about businesses and unknown people obtaining information about their web activities; 86.5% believed individuals should control who sees their personal information; 50.4% felt third-party firms collect excessive amounts from online library users. From Lund's analysis of 1,000 sampled public libraries: 44.6% had a data privacy policy, roughly two-thirds updated within five years, 15.9% last updated before 2016. From O'Brien et al.'s study of 279 academic library websites: limited HTTPS adoption, widespread Google Analytics and Tag Manager use, few secure connections to Google or IP anonymization. From Marino's study of 124 ARL member homepages: about one-third featured third-party tracking cookies.

Their argument is that libraries have become "digital consumers" — running the same network-based data infrastructure as commercial providers while claiming a non-commercial ethic — and that difference in intent does not neutralize risk. They pay particular attention to the vendor layer, noting patrons often cannot tell whether they are using an institutional service or an external site. The article closes by proposing **F-TechEthix**, a framework with four constructs: User-Centric Information Services, Privacy and Data Protection, Technological Integration and Ethical Practices, and Global Network Compliance (aimed at cross-border transfers and agreements with publishers and aggregators).

:::{tip} Why it's here
This is the block's non-US anchor and its O3 contribution. POPIA is not GDPR and is not FERPA; watching a library privacy analysis run through a third statutory regime shows which professional commitments are portable and which are artifacts of US law. It is also the only assigned reading with survey percentages for patron attitudes — useful when the simulation's community-demands branch asks you to weigh privacy against convenience.
:::

> "Consequently, library privacy is a crucial aspect, because of the 'chilling effect', in which users become aware or suspect that they are being monitored and change their behaviour as a result." (Section: Libraries, librarians and privacy, p. 2)

## 5. Chin et al. (2023)

**Patron privacy protections in public libraries: IT professionals' points of view**

> Chin, C.-L., Wang, T., Wang, E.-H., & Bashir, M. (2023). Patron privacy protections in public libraries: IT professionals' points of view. Journal of Intellectual Freedom & Privacy, 8(3), 28–39.

**Find it.** Perusall/Canvas. (*Note:* the *Journal of Intellectual Freedom & Privacy* is currently dormant and its ALA-hosted platform is unreliable; use the Perusall copy. Pagination confirmed from the Fall 2023 issue PDF in the project.)

**In the course project.** `Chin.2023.PatronPrivacy.pdf`

A focus-group study: ten online sessions in summer 2021, thirty-three IT professionals affiliated with US public libraries, recruited through a mailing list of over 12,500 state public library staff plus association working groups including REFORMA, BCALA, AILA, APALA, and CALA. Participants served rural (5), suburban (13), and urban (14) communities plus consortia; 36% reported serving underserved communities. Titles skewed administrative: seven IT directors or heads, eleven managers, three coordinators, three technicians, six librarians, three non-IT department heads. The authors present it as the first study focused on library IT professionals' views of patron privacy, following a prior survey of 700+ librarians and administrators (Wang et al., 2023).

On **pressing concerns**, participants named systems they cannot see into — "It is unknown what apps on the computer are tracking which interactions patrons might have on the public machines" — legacy systems sending SIP traffic over unencrypted ports, and camera hardware that may carry facial recognition they cannot verify is disabled. They also named their own libraries' practices: collecting more than needed, losing control of data shared with vendors and consortia, paper forms left in printers. On **technologies in use**: Deep Freeze and session management, VPNs, encryption, firewalls, barcodes replacing patron IDs, session purging, encrypted print jobs dumped nightly, locked data centers, audits, staff training. On **most serious risks**: breachable patron databases, unencrypted transmission, remote printing over Wi-Fi, named commercial services (OverDrive, OpenAthens, AWS, Google, SIP2), shared staff logins, outdated policies. On **changes needed**: anonymization by default, reduced third-party wireless tracking, patron disclosure statements, patron education, and repeatedly, that the necessary change is cultural and budgetary rather than technical.

The finding with the sharpest ethical edge is the customer-service tension. Participants described being unable to help low-digital-literacy patrons enter their own data without violating policy, and conceded that staff follow policy "flexibly," especially in small communities where staff and patrons know each other. More than half were unfamiliar with the ALA Library Privacy Checklist; among those who knew it, most called it a good guideline but "not implementable."

:::{tip} Why it's here
Every other reading this week describes what should be true. This one describes what is true, from the vantage of the people who would have to make it true. It also inverts the usual moral geometry: here the patron is often the one asking the library to collect and expose more.
:::

> "One of the challenges that we're fighting often is that our patrons don't take privacy as seriously as they should, and so oftentimes they're willing to take risks that we, as the library just can't do so. Balancing security with convenience is closely tied to that." (RQ1 findings, p. 33)
