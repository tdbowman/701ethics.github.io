# How to Use This Book 🧭

A few minutes here will save you hours later.

---

## The Weekly Rhythm

Each week of the course has three pages in this book:

1. **The week's overview page**: what the week is about, what you will learn, the big questions, the readings table, and what is due.
2. **The Readings**: an entry for every assigned text, with the full citation, where to find it, what it actually argues, and why it sits in this week.
3. **Concepts & Questions**: the week's key terms, the annotation prompts to bring into Perusall, and how the week connects to the scenario and the final essay.

Read the overview first, skim the reading entries, then go read the actual texts. Come back to Concepts & Questions when you are ready to annotate.

## Reading the Readings

Not every text asks for the same attention:

- **Codes, manifestos, and interpretations** are short and operative. Read them the way a lawyer reads a statute: slowly, noticing exactly what is required, permitted, and merely encouraged.
- **Empirical articles** should be read for method first. Who was studied, how many, and what could this design not have found?
- **Critiques and essays** are arguments. Find the claim, find the evidence, and find the move you would contest.
- **Reports and toolkits** are for skimming with purpose. Know what is in them and where to look when you need it.

## Annotating in Perusall

Reading Engagement is 20% of your grade and it is scored on the quality of conversation, not the quantity of highlights. The prompts at the end of each week are written to earn it honestly: each one points at a specific passage and takes a position someone could reasonably dispute. Responding to a classmate counts for more than restating the author.

## About the Scores

Your scenario performances and the capstone report generate numbers. Those numbers come from Dr. Bowman's own reading of these materials. They are feedback, not an official assessment metric, and the final essay explicitly asks you to say what is wrong with them. Read them the way you would read any algorithmic judgment about a person: as an argument with premises.
