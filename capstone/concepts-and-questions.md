## 🗝️ Key Concepts

| Term | What it means |
|------|---------------|
| **Construct validity** | whether an instrument measures what it claims to; the central question for any dimension score, because "ethical reasoning" is a construct and a branching-scenario choice is not the same thing. |
| **Proxy measure** | an observable stand-in for something unobservable; your scenario selections are proxies for professional judgment, and every proxy has a characterizable gap. |
| **Reflexivity** | accounting for your own position in your analysis. You are simultaneously the object being scored, the analyst of the scoring, and the person whose grade depends on the analysis. Say so rather than pretending to neutrality. |
| **Actionable commitment** | a statement specific enough that someone else could tell whether you kept it; it names a behavior, a context, and a way of knowing. "Advocate for privacy" is not one; "in any vendor evaluation I participate in, ask in writing what patron data the product retains and for how long, and record the answer" is. |
| **Unresolved tension** | a genuine conflict between two things you hold, stated without resolution; not a weakness to confess but an accurate description of a position with more than one commitment in it. |

All terms from all sixteen weeks are collected in the [Glossary](../glossary.md).

## 💬 Annotation & Discussion Prompts

These are written for **Perusall**. Each one points at something specific and each one is arguable, which is the point: annotation credit comes from advancing a conversation, not from summarizing a paragraph.

1. Before opening your report, write 150 words predicting your composite score and philosophy label, and why. Afterward, write 150 words on the gap. That gap, not the score, is your best evidence about your own self-knowledge, and Varlejs (2016, §1.2.1) is the citation for why self-assessment is unreliable in exactly this way.
2. List every input the integrator has: which JSON files, from which blocks, capturing which choices. Then list what it lacks: your reasoning, revisions, reflection prompts, Perusall annotations, everything learned after each scenario closed. Write one paragraph on what a model built only from the first list can support.
3. Take one dimension score and reverse-engineer it. Which decisions plausibly fed it? What weighting would have to be true for the number you got? Where does your reconstruction fail? Write the specific question you would have to ask the instructor to close the gap.
4. Run your report through Floridi and Cowls's five principles, one paragraph each. Two will produce nothing useful. The other three are the spine of Part 1.
5. Find the one recorded choice you now think was wrong. Write 200 words on what you would do differently and what changed your mind, naming the reading, block, and passage. This paragraph is the hinge between Parts 1 and 2.
6. Write your philosophy label as a sentence in your own words, then the sentence you would have written for yourself. Mark every difference. Baer (2025) will help you notice how you *feel* about it, which is data you may use.
7. Draft three actionable commitments. For each, name the person, budget line, or policy that would have to exist for you to keep it, and cite the reading that tells you whether it does. Cut the one you cannot ground.

## 🔗 Connections

- **Scenario:** Each performance becomes a different kind of evidence. Block 1 (case analysis, ethics decision tree, professional philosophy) is your baseline, the position you held before the course argued with you. Block 2 (data ethics simulation, privacy policy, digital divide assessment) is where a decision first carried a numeric threshold, and where 75/100 with unlimited retries was required, so your Block 2 record may reflect persistence rather than judgment, a construct-validity problem you can name precisely. Block 3 (DEI framework, social justice and equity plan) is where commitments became institutional rather than personal. Block 4 (choose-your-own ethics adventure) is your AI decision-making under branching pressure, and the block you must cite. Block 5, the integrator, is the fifth performance: the one where you are the object rather than the agent. Treat that reversal as the essay's structural insight.
- **Final essay:** A workable shape for 1,800–2,400 words. *Introduction* (150–200): what the report claimed about you and the argument you will make about that claim. *Part 1, framing* (200–250): what kind of object this report is, and the two-part explicability test you will apply. *Part 1, critique* (600–750): two or three named problems, each grounded in evidence from your own report, at least one carrying your required Block 4 citation; concede one point the report got right. *Hinge* (150–200): what survives the critique. *Part 2, philosophy* (350–450): your position, in your voice, grounded in readings from at least two blocks. *Part 2, commitments* (300–400): two or three, each checkable, each with a named condition of possibility. *Unresolved tension* (150–200): one, stated honestly and left open. *Conclusion* (100–150). Reference list separate from the word count.
- **Threads back:** Strongest cross-block pairings for an argument about algorithmic self-assessment. **Floridi & Cowls + Cox's eight scenarios (Block 4)**: abstract principles tested against concrete cases, exactly what the integrator claims to have done to you. **Varlejs + your Block 1 professional philosophy** applies the unreliability of self-assessment to the self-assessment you performed in week two. **Rosenthal + the Block 2 data ethics simulation**: a precise, confident, unfalsifiable number in both places, and a demonstration that precision is not accuracy. **Baer + the ARL or ALA AI guidance (Block 4)** reads feeling rules against the documents that encode them. **UNESCO's human oversight principle + your Block 2 privacy policy**: responsibility must always attach to a person, applied to a system that has assigned you a label. Any two will carry an essay; the first and third together will carry a very good one.
