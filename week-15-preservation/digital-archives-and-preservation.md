# Week 15: Digital Archives & Preservation 🗃️

**Block 5: Synthesis & Professional Integration | November 30 – December 6, 2026**

---

*Keeping bits safe is arithmetic, and the arithmetic is worse than you think.*

Your capstone report and synthesis form are due Sunday, December 6. Plan accordingly. These readings are short enough to do properly alongside the capstone, and one of them will change how you think about what the capstone is.

Preservation operates on a timescale nobody's career can span, and that produces a particular kind of ethics. When Rosenthal asks how many copies, in storage systems of what reliability, you need for a given probability that data survives, he is asking a question that looks like engineering and turns out to be epistemology. His answer is that you cannot know. The obstacle is not expense or computational difficulty. No evidence an organization could afford to gather would settle the question. That conclusion, reached by arithmetic rather than hand-wringing, is the most bracing thing you will read all term.

### 🎯 What You'll Learn

- Learn what preservation actually costs, in money and in engineering
- Read AI-readiness as an appraisal problem rather than a technical upgrade
- Document what your collection excludes, and treat that documentation as part of the record
- Connect personal data literacy to the ethics of keeping things

### 🧠 Big Questions to Consider

- If Rosenthal is right that we cannot know whether our storage is good enough, what is the honest thing to tell a donor or a community whose records you have accepted for permanent preservation?
- Colavizza and Jaillant ask archives to document what was excluded from digitization and why. Who is served by that documentation, and what does an institution risk by producing it?
- When does preservation become surveillance? What principle distinguishes keeping a community's records from keeping a record of a community?
