## 1. Varlejs (2016)

**IFLA guidelines for continuing professional development: Principles and best practices (2nd ed.)**

> Varlejs, J. (2016). IFLA guidelines for continuing professional development: Principles and best practices (2nd ed.). International Federation of Library Associations and Institutions. https://repository.ifla.org/handle/20.500.14598/1111

**Find it.** https://www.ifla.org/files/assets/cpdwl/guidelines/ifla-guidelines-for-continuing-professional-development.pdf (open access, CC-BY 4.0)

**In the course project.** `iflaguidelinesforcontinuingprofessionaldevelopment.pdf`

The organizing move is structural. Rather than one undifferentiated list of good practices, the document assigns obligations by role across five chapters: the learner, the employer, professional associations and government bodies, LIS degree-granting programs, and all providers. Each states a principle, gives a rationale, then enumerates best practices. The effect is to make it hard to locate responsibility in only one place. When a librarian's skills go stale, you can ask which of five parties failed.

Chapter 1 places primary responsibility on the individual and specifies what that means: regular self-assessment against published competency lists, participation in performance appraisal, environmental scanning, a written personal learning plan, and a rule of precedence — you address what your *current* position requires before preparing for the next one. Varlejs is careful about the limits of this. She quotes research finding that professionals tend to name as weak those areas they use infrequently and rarely perceive deficiency in tasks they do daily, and cites evidence that self-ratings vary wildly with individual self-awareness. Self-assessment is necessary and unreliable — a claim you will want to reuse in the final essay.

Chapter 2 turns to employers and gets specific in a way most professional guidance does not: a designated staff development coordinator, regular needs assessment tied to institutional mission, documentation of participation feeding into compensation and promotion, a minimum of 0.5%–1.0% of institutional budget earmarked for staff development (a figure inherited from the IFLA/UNESCO public library guidelines, which the text calls "truly minimal"), and roughly 10% of work hours available for learning. Chapters 3 and 4 extend obligations to associations — recognition and certification systems, coordinated provision, advocacy for policies guaranteeing access to continuing education — and to LIS faculty, asked to model lifelong learning and research the field's own gaps.

Chapter 5 governs anyone who provides learning: instructional design grounded in needs assessment and SMART objectives, instructors chosen for teaching ability as well as subject expertise, explicit attention to "transfer of training" from event to workplace, and evaluation. For evaluation it adopts Kirkpatrick's four levels — reaction, learning, behavior, results — and concedes that most providers reach only the first two. The document also introduces the metaphor doing the most work in the text: every practitioner is part of a *learning ecosystem* of colleagues, employers, associations, vendors, universities, and government agencies, each of which can enable or discourage learning.

:::{tip} Why it's here
This is the only document in the course that treats your own competence as a resource somebody has to fund and maintain, which makes it the bridge between "here are the ethics" and "here is what it takes to keep being able to practice them." It also gives you a vocabulary — needs assessment, competency gap, personal learning plan, transfer of training, learning ecosystem — that will make your Part 2 commitments read like a professional plan rather than a New Year's resolution.
:::

> "The individual library and information professional is primarily responsible for pursuing ongoing learning that constantly improves knowledge and skills." (Chapter 1, §1.1, p. 20)

## 2. IFLA CPDWL Section (2025)

**IFLA guidelines for continuing professional development: Principles and best practices — Part 2: Guidelines for CPD in the online**

> IFLA Continuing Professional Development and Workplace Learning Section. (2025). IFLA guidelines for continuing professional development: Principles and best practices — Part 2: Guidelines for CPD in the online context. International Federation of Library Associations and Institutions.

**Find it.** https://www.ifla.org/news/empowering-library-workers-in-the-digital-age-revised-ifla-cpd-guidelines/ (announcement, 28 May 2025; document linked from the CPDWL Section page)

A citation correction first, because the syllabus history is muddled. The *second edition* of the IFLA CPD Guidelines is the 2016 Varlejs document above — its own title page says so, identifying it as the second edition of the 2006 *Continuing Professional Development: Principles and Best Practices*. The 2025 item is neither a third edition nor a replacement; it is a supplementary Part 2 on continuing professional development in online environments, announced 28 May 2025 and developed by a CPDWL working group co-chaired by Man Yi Helen Chan. If you cite "IFLA CPD Guidelines, 2nd ed. (2025)" you will be citing a document that does not exist under that description. Cite Varlejs (2016) for the core guidelines and the 2025 Part 2 separately.

Per IFLA's own description, Part 2 addresses three concerns: quality assurance for online learning together with equitable access and inclusivity; collaboration among the four stakeholder groups the 2016 document names; and emerging practices including AI-driven personalization and open educational resources. This extends rather than decorates, because the 2016 guidelines flagged the online question as unresolved — Part II of that document, "Future Concerns," asks explicitly whether the principles defining quality face-to-face hold online, and notes that language, bandwidth, and awareness remain barriers even as offerings proliferate. Treat the AI-driven personalization piece with the skepticism this course has trained into you: a proposal to let an algorithm infer your competency gaps is structurally the same proposal the Block 5 integrator makes about your ethics. That symmetry is worth a paragraph in your essay.

:::{tip} Why it's here
Because the correction itself is instructive — guidance documents get revised, superseded, and mis-cited, and part of ethical practice is knowing which version you are standing on. It also puts AI-driven personalization of professional learning on the table in the same week you read UNESCO on human oversight.
:::

## 3. UNESCO (2021)

**Recommendation on the ethics of artificial intelligence**

> UNESCO. (2021). Recommendation on the ethics of artificial intelligence. United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization. https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000381137

**Find it.** https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000381137 (open access)

**In the course project.** `UNESCORecommendationAI.pdf`

Adopted at the 41st session of the UNESCO General Conference on 23 November 2021, this is the first global standard-setting instrument on AI ethics, and its structure matters as much as its content. It declines to define AI — any single definition "would need to change over time" — and instead addresses the ethically relevant features of AI systems, framing everything around the *AI system life cycle*: research, design, development, deployment, use, maintenance, operation, trade, financing, monitoring, validation, end-of-use, and termination. Every obligation attaches to a stage of that cycle rather than to a technology.

Section III sets out four **values**: respect, protection and promotion of human rights and fundamental freedoms and human dignity; environment and ecosystem flourishing; ensuring diversity and inclusiveness; and living in peaceful, just and interconnected societies. It then sets out ten **principles**: proportionality and do no harm; safety and security; fairness and non-discrimination; sustainability; right to privacy and data protection; human oversight and determination; transparency and explainability; responsibility and accountability; awareness and literacy; and multi-stakeholder and adaptive governance and collaboration. Several have teeth. Under proportionality the text states flatly that "AI systems should not be used for social scoring or mass surveillance purposes." Under human oversight it holds that responsibility must always be attributable to natural or legal persons, and that AI systems must not be given legal personality.

Section IV is where it departs from most ethics statements: eleven **areas of policy action** — ethical impact assessment; ethical governance and stewardship; data policy; development and international cooperation; environment and ecosystems; gender; culture; education and research; communication and information; economy and labour; and health and social well-being. Policy Area 1 asks Member States to build frameworks for ethical impact assessment identifying impacts on human rights, on marginalized people, on labour rights and ecosystems, and to make those assessments transparent and open to the public where appropriate. Policy Area 7 encourages museums, galleries, libraries and archives to use AI to surface their collections; Policy Area 9 asks for investment in media and information literacy sufficient to counter disinformation; Policy Area 5 requires assessment of carbon footprint, energy consumption, and raw material extraction across the life cycle, and states that where impacts are disproportionate, "AI should not be used."

The document is candid that its own values and principles will conflict. Paragraph 11 says so directly: tensions are expected, and resolving them requires contextual assessment, proportionality, stakeholder engagement, and impact assessment. Note also that Member States apply it "on a voluntary basis" — this has moral and diplomatic force, not the force of a treaty.

:::{tip} Why it's here
This is your global-scale counterweight to the American professional documents that have anchored most of the course, and the source to reach for when you need to argue that a library's technology decision has implications beyond the library. It is also the most complete public statement anywhere of what explainability is supposed to mean — precisely what you will be demanding from your own capstone report.
:::

> "It may be the case that sometimes humans would choose to rely on AI systems for reasons of efficacy, but the decision to cede control in limited contexts remains that of humans, as humans can resort to AI systems in decision-making and acting, but an AI system can never replace ultimate human responsibility and accountability." (§III.2, "Human oversight and determination," para. 36)

## 4. ALA (2026)

**FAQ: Executive order targeting IMLS**

> American Library Association. (2026). FAQ: Executive order targeting IMLS. https://www.ala.org/faq-executive-order-targeting-imls

**Find it.** https://www.ala.org/faq-executive-order-targeting-imls (open access; last updated April 9, 2026)

This is an advocacy document and you should read it as one, which is not the same as discounting it. The FAQ addresses Executive Order 14238, issued March 14, 2025, which directed the elimination of the Institute of Museum and Library Services along with six other federal agencies to the maximum extent of the law, instructed affected agencies to reduce services and personnel to the statutory minimum, and directed the Office of Management and Budget to reject IMLS budget requests other than funds needed to shut the agency down.

ALA's account of the stakes runs along two lines. The first is proportional: IMLS appropriations represent less than 0.003% of the federal budget, against which the FAQ sets a concrete inventory of what that money buys — talking-book and braille services for readers with disabilities, interlibrary loan infrastructure, statewide database licensing for students and health professionals, bookmobiles, literacy programming. The second is procedural: because Library Services and Technology Act funds flow through state library agencies as Grants to States, an interruption at the federal level propagates into every state's library ecosystem at once, including the continuing education and train-the-trainer programs the IFLA Guidelines assume exist. The FAQ's action section is unambiguous — contact members of Congress, write to local newspapers, share community impact stories, join or renew ALA membership, sign up for advocacy alerts. As of its April 9, 2026 update it records that litigation in April 2026 reversed the order's implementation, which is itself worth noticing: this document has been revised as the legal situation moved. Check the current version when you cite it. A live advocacy FAQ is not a stable text.

Read alongside Chapter 3 of the IFLA Guidelines, which asks associations to "advocate for policies and regulations that ensure library/information staff have access to continuing education" and cites IMLS grant programs as an example, this FAQ is that recommendation being executed under pressure.

:::{tip} Why it's here
Because ethical leadership has a budget line, and this is the week you see it. It also gives you a live case in which a professional association takes an openly political position — the sharpest available test of the neutrality arguments you worked through in Block 1.
:::
