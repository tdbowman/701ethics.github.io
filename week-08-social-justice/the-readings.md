## 1. Drabinski (2013)

**Queering the catalog: Queer theory and the politics of correction**

> Drabinski, E. (2013). Queering the catalog: Queer theory and the politics of correction. The Library Quarterly, 83(2), 94–111. https://doi.org/10.1086/669547

**Find it.** https://doi.org/10.1086/669547. Open-access author copy: https://digitalcommons.liu.edu/brooklyn_libfacpubs/9/

**In the course project.** `Queering the Catalog_ Queer Theory and the Politics of Correction.pdf`

Drabinski opens by granting the critical cataloging tradition its victories. She traces it to Sanford Berman's discovery, cataloging at the University of Zambia in the late 1960s, that the LCSH heading *Kafirs*, merely descriptive to American catalogers, was a virulent racist epithet in Zambian use. That encounter made materially evident what philosophy and anthropology had argued abstractly: subject headings are not a pure objective language, and "where and when and by whom subject headings are used makes all the difference in terms of meaning." From Berman through Marshall, Foskett, Wolf, Greenblatt, Olson, Roberto, and Freedman, librarians have petitioned, published, and organized, and won real changes, including moving materials on homosexuality out from under *Sexual deviations* in 1972.

Her critique is that this tradition defines the problem functionally and so arrives at functional solutions: things are cataloged incorrectly and, with enough activist pressure on SACO, can be cataloged correctly. Queer theory denies the premise. She distinguishes queer theory from lesbian and gay studies: the latter recuperative, seeking stable identities in a record that omitted them; the former, via Halperin, Sedgwick, Butler, and Foucault, asking how those identities came discursively into being. From there she locates the trouble "in the project of fixity itself." Categories are contingent and mutually constitutive: Butler's point that claiming an identity produces the outside it excludes; Keilty's point that "queer's non-normativity relies on norms as a precondition." Her compressed example is a person who needs the heading *Lesbian* to find books about herself and simultaneously experiences it as wrong for her.

She gives the historical proof by counterexample. In 1972 Joan Marshall argued *Negro women* was an improvement over *Mammies*; by 2012, activist catalogers would target *Negro women* for removal. Corrections are "always contingent and never final." Worse, correction is counterproductive in one respect: by smoothing the ruptures where ideology shows through, it makes the structure look objective. Her alternative relocates the work rather than leaving users unaided. Public services librarians should invite users into the same dialogic engagement critical catalogers already practice, leading a user searching for *genderqueer* or *aggressive* to related materials while discussing why their language is invisible in the structure. She endorses Olson's technical proposals (exposing broader and related terms, user tagging, local thesauri, varied citation order) and Heidi Jacobs's pedagogy of "teaching the conflicts." The catalog becomes a teaching text about how power constructs the categories through which we are permitted to be legible.

:::{tip} Why it's here
Drabinski is the most theoretically demanding reading in Block 3 and the one that most repays slow reading. It is also frequently mis-cited: as 2012, or with the LIU repository date attached to the wrong page range. The canonical citation is volume 83, issue 2, pages 94–111, DOI 10.1086/669547. Beyond the catalog, it gives you a general argument about whether biased systems should be repaired or exposed, which is the question Block 4 asks about algorithms.
:::

> "In fact, the political focus on correcting classification structure and subject language solidifies the idea that the classification structure is in fact objective and does in fact tell the truth, the core fictions—from a queer perspective—that allow the hegemony of a universalized classification structure to persist." (p. 104)

## 2. Bailund et al. (2026)

**Seeking approval, confronting objectivity: Neutrality in the Library of Congress subject headings approval process**

> Bailund, A., Tomaras, D., Cronquist, M., & Gross, T. (2026, March 18). Seeking approval, confronting objectivity: Neutrality in the Library of Congress subject headings approval process. In the Library with the Lead Pipe.

**Find it.** https://www.inthelibrarywiththeleadpipe.org/2026/seeking-approval/ (open access, peer reviewed)

Where Drabinski analyzes the headings that exist, Bailund, Tomaras, Cronquist, and Gross analyze the ones that do not. Their study examines proposed Library of Congress subject headings rejected over nearly twenty years and asks what reasoning the rejections rest on. The recurring justification is neutrality. Their first finding is that neutrality is nowhere defined in the Library of Congress's own documentation, despite doing the decisive work in these decisions.

A second finding: rejections form a pattern rather than a scatter. Terms naming prejudice and structural harm (their examples include *White flight* and *Toxic masculinity*) are disproportionately declined, as are terms describing marginalized experience. An undefined neutrality thus operates as a one-directional filter: vocabulary that would make injustice nameable is screened out, while the absence of such vocabulary is not itself registered as a departure from neutrality. Neutrality, on this reading, preserves the status quo rather than holding a balance.

The third move is normative. They set the neutrality standard against the profession's explicit commitments to social responsibility and equity and argue these cannot both govern; they propose replacing neutrality with equity as the criterion for judging heading proposals. They also raise a governance problem easy to miss: the Library of Congress holds unilateral authority over a vocabulary used by thousands of institutions worldwide, with no formal mechanism for affected communities to participate. Their recommendations follow: external advisory groups, prioritizing community voices in vocabulary development, removing neutrality language from policy documents, and aligning practice with the *Cataloguing Code of Ethics*.

:::{tip} Why it's here
It is the 2026 counterweight to Drabinski, and reading the two together is the intellectual work of the week. Drabinski argues the corrective project misunderstands its object; Bailund et al. accept the project and argue its *criterion* is the problem. It also brings the neutrality debate, the central live disagreement in contemporary American librarianship and a recurring one in Blocks 4 and 5, into a domain concrete enough to argue about with evidence rather than slogans.
:::

> "It considers the place of neutrality in libraries more generally and argues that equity, rather than neutrality, is the appropriate lens for judging subject heading proposals." (In Brief)

## 3. Morales et al. (2014)

**Diversity, social justice, and the future of libraries**

> Morales, M., Knowles, E. C., & Bourg, C. (2014). Diversity, social justice, and the future of libraries. portal: Libraries and the Academy, 14(3), 439–451. https://doi.org/10.1353/pla.2014.0017

**Find it.** https://doi.org/10.1353/pla.2014.0017. Author copy: https://chrisbourg.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/07/14-3morales.pdf

**In the course project.** `Morales.2014.Diversity.pdf`

The essay opens on a contradiction: ALA affirmed diversity as a core value in 1999, and librarianship "remains a painfully homogenous profession." Its central conceptual contribution is to separate two things the profession routinely conflates. Diversity describes the mix of individual characteristics in a group. Social justice "is generally understood to refer to the ability of all people to fully benefit from social and economic progress and to participate equally in democratic societies"; it "addresses power and privilege on a structural level, as well as at the level of mere representation." They are explicit that increased diversity does not guarantee a justice commitment.

They document the demographic case carefully. Census projections through 2060 show the Hispanic population more than doubling and the United States becoming majority-nonwhite in 2043. Against that, ALA's Diversity Counts data for 2009–2010 found 88 percent of credentialed librarians were white; 2012 ALISE data showed 71 percent of students in ALA-accredited programs were white. They review the existing interventions: Spectrum, with more than 800 scholars at the time of writing plus a doctoral fellowship; ARL's Career Enhancement and Leadership and Career Development Programs; LAMP, the iSchool Inclusion Initiative, Knowledge River, Project IDOL. Their conclusion is that the programs are laudable but that aggregate impact "is constrained by the small number of participants and scholarships available."

The most vivid section is two first-person accounts of classification as structural injustice. Myrna Morales, researching the Young Lords Party, found nothing under "political organization" in the *Reader's Guide*; a "(twisted) gut feeling" sent her to *gangs*, where the YLP was. Chris Bourg found Randy Shilts's definitive history of gays and lesbians in the U.S. military subclassified under "Minorities, women, etc. in armed forces," shelved between two books of gay military erotica, and absent from the military history stacks. These are not anecdotes about offense; they demonstrate that classification determines who can find what. They extend the argument to collection development, warning that patron-driven acquisition inherits publishing's biases (white writers wrote 90 percent of books reviewed by *The New York Times* in 2011) and yields "an even more biased version of the scholarly record preserved and made available to future generations." Their positive model is the Yvonne Pappenheim Library on Racism in Boston, a largely volunteer-run community library that works by association and referral, sends a weekly "Resource for Justice" email, hosts activist groups, and collaborates with the Boston Radical Reference Collective and the Progressive Librarians Guild. Notably, they report that in Boston "the neutrality conversation ... often segues into a conversation on race, racism and racial diversity in the profession." The essay also performs its argument formally: three authors from different positions wrote it in their own voices to model collaboration across "structurally designed segregated social networks" and to leave their disagreements visible.

:::{tip} Why it's here
It supplies the definitional distinction the whole block depends on, and links Week 7's demographic problem to this week's structural one through the classification examples. It is also the reading that most directly connects academic librarianship to community and grassroots practice, which matters if your career runs outside the academy.
:::

> "As many have said in the past, no profession becomes and remains this homogenous by accident." (p. 443)

## 4. Winberry & Bishop (2021)

**Documenting social justice in library and information science research: A literature review**

> Winberry, J., & Bishop, B. W. (2021). Documenting social justice in library and information science research: A literature review. Journal of Documentation, 77(3), 743–754. https://doi.org/10.1108/JD-08-2020-0136

**Find it.** https://doi.org/10.1108/JD-08-2020-0136. Repository copy: https://trace.tennessee.edu/utk_infosciepubs/448/

**In the course project.** `Winberry.2021.DocumentingSocialJustice.pdf`

This is a systematic literature review, and its value is that it tells you the shape of a field rather than arguing a position within it. Winberry and Bishop searched LISTA and LISS for "social justice," screened 2,372 initial records to 754 academic results, removed 342 duplicates, and after temporal and content screening arrived at 247 records published before January 2020. Their inclusion rule is deliberately narrow: a study counts if its authors used the phrase "social justice" to conceptualize, explain, or situate the work, explicitly or implicitly. They acknowledge the cost, since research serving marginalized populations without the term is excluded, and defend the rule as the only way to get a tractable sample.

On timing, they find social justice appearing sporadically before 2000, rising steadily, spiking with a 322 percent increase in 2014–2015 over 2012–2013, peaking in 2016–2017, then declining noticeably in 2018–2019. They attribute part of the rise to special issues and social-justice-centered serials, and are careful not to claim the peak is permanent. Contextually, archives are the most common setting, followed by academic libraries, public libraries, LIS education, community settings, youth spaces, online settings, data contexts, and scholarly communications; older adults and Indigenous communities are conspicuously underrepresented.

Their conceptual contribution is a two-part typology. *Indirect action* is "steps necessary for making change possible": theoretical and analytical work, conceptual frameworks, curricular arguments. *Direct action* is "specific steps to implement change" (empirical and procedural work that alters an actual institution). They find nearly twice as much indirect as direct action, note archival research skews heavily toward direct action, and argue both are necessary rather than ranking them, citing bell hooks's "thinking is an action" against a simple planning/doing binary. Their discussion raises a practitioner-facing point worth pausing on: direct-action research is what practitioners can use, and much of it sits behind paywalls smaller libraries and non-profits cannot scale, so open access is itself a condition for social justice research. They frame the field as at a crossroads, asking whether it will mature or "prove to be the buzz term or fad when no longer in vogue."

*Note on the 2026 revision:* the syllabus revision intended to replace this article with a 2025 Winberry publication. No 2025 Winberry article covering this ground could be verified. His verifiable 2025 work is on aging services information, and his 2026 work addresses information marginalization, LIS education in an aging society, and Friends-of-the-Library advocacy against book bans. The 2021 review is therefore assigned; see GOING DEEPER for the verified 2026 items.

:::{tip} Why it's here
It gives you the map. After four readings that each argue a position, this one shows where those positions sit in a literature of 247 items, what the field has neglected, and what kind of contribution you are making when you write. The indirect/direct action distinction is immediately usable: it is the question to ask of your own action plan and of any equity initiative you are handed in practice.
:::

> "[T]his study's findings suggest that social justice research contributions are either indirect action (i.e. steps necessary for making change possible) or direct action (i.e. specific steps to implement change)." (p. 748)

## 5. McEvoy (2024)

**Divisive politics and threats to academic libraries**

> McEvoy, K. (2024). Divisive politics and threats to academic libraries. The Political Librarian, 7(2). https://doi.org/10.7936/pollib.8947

**Find it.** https://journals.library.wustl.edu/pollib/article/id/8947/ (open access)

McEvoy's essay is short, argumentative, and addressed to academic library leadership. Her premise is that the political pressure campaign already visible against school and public libraries (book bans, criminalization efforts targeting library workers and educators) is not confined to those sectors and is arriving in higher education. Her prediction concerns form as much as incidence: attacks on academic librarians will be less visible than those on school librarians, so the damage will register as self-censorship, quietly eliminated programming, and funding scrutiny rather than as public challenges with names attached.

She situates this within wider pressures on the humanities, on DEI initiatives, and on research freedom, alongside disinformation campaigns that target the credibility of information literacy work itself. Her framing of the environment as an information war is deliberate: it lets her argue that librarians are not incidental casualties but the profession with the relevant expertise, and that institutions under pressure should be turning toward their libraries rather than away.

Note what kind of text this is: an opinion essay in a journal about library policy and advocacy, not an empirical study. Read it for its claims about mechanism, especially the claim that self-censorship is the primary damage vector, and test those claims against what you can observe rather than treating its predictions as findings.

:::{tip} Why it's here
Every other reading in this block assumes an institution at least nominally free to pursue equity commitments. McEvoy asks what happens when that assumption fails. She also makes the neutrality debate concrete: an institution under political pressure has a powerful incentive to declare itself neutral, and Bailund et al. have just shown what neutrality does when it becomes a decision criterion. Read them in sequence.
:::
