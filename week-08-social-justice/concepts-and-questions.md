## 🗝️ Key Concepts

| Term | What it means |
|------|---------------|
| **Diversity vs. social justice** | Morales, Knowles, and Bourg's governing distinction: diversity describes a group's composition; social justice addresses power and privilege structurally, so a diverse staff running an unchanged system has achieved one and not the other. |
| **The politics of correction** | Drabinski's name for the critical cataloging project of fixing biased headings, which she argues concedes that a universal, correct system is possible and desirable. |
| **Contingency of categories** | Drabinski's core claim, via Foucault and Butler: identity categories are discursively produced and historically bound, so every correction is provisional and will itself become a target. |
| **Rupture** | Drabinski's term for a visibly biased heading or classification that exposes the catalog's ideology to a user, and which correction erases along with the offense. |
| **Dialogic engagement** | Drabinski's alternative to correction: teaching users at the reference desk and in the classroom to read the catalog as a biased text rather than navigate it as a neutral one. |
| **Neutrality as a decision criterion** | from Bailund et al.: an undefined standard invoked to reject heading proposals, functioning as a one-directional filter against vocabulary that names injustice. |
| **Indirect action / direct action** | Winberry and Bishop's typology: steps that make change possible versus specific steps that implement it. |
| **Structural vs. individual remedy** | the recurring choice across the week: fix the system, or fix the case in front of you. Morales et al. and Bailund et al. argue structurally; Drabinski deliberately relocates the remedy to the individual encounter. |
| **Self-censorship** | McEvoy's predicted primary damage mechanism under political pressure: harm that leaves no challenge record because the material or program is never offered. |

All terms from all sixteen weeks are collected in the [Glossary](../glossary.md).

## 💬 Annotation & Discussion Prompts

These are written for **Perusall**. Each one points at something specific and each one is arguable, which is the point: annotation credit comes from advancing a conversation, not from summarizing a paragraph.

1. Drabinski argues on p. 104 that correcting headings "solidifies the idea that the classification structure is in fact objective." Annotate this from the position of a user harmed by the uncorrected heading. Whose education is served by leaving the rupture visible, and is that trade defensible?
2. Bailund et al. propose replacing neutrality with equity as the criterion for judging heading proposals. Construct the strongest objection a cataloger *committed to equity* might still raise, then answer it.
3. Morales, Knowles, and Bourg report the Young Lords Party was findable only under *gangs*. Annotate that passage with Drabinski's argument. Would Drabinski have wanted that heading corrected, and does her framework give a satisfying answer to Morales?
4. Winberry and Bishop found nearly twice as much indirect- as direct-action research, and note direct-action work is what practitioners can use. Annotate their discussion with an argument about incentives: who benefits from the current ratio?
5. McEvoy predicts self-censorship rather than overt censorship as the main damage vector. Propose a detection method. What evidence would demonstrate self-censorship, given that its defining feature is the absence of a record?
6. Morales et al. note that in Boston "the neutrality conversation ... often segues into a conversation on race, racism and racial diversity in the profession." Is that segue an intellectual advance or a change of subject? Defend your answer using Bailund et al.
7. Drabinski distinguishes queer theory from lesbian and gay studies by saying the latter asks what homosexuality *is* and the former what it *does*. Apply this distinction to a category outside sexuality: disability, immigration status, or race. Does it survive the transfer?

## 🔗 Connections

- **Scenario:** In "Building Inclusive Spaces," the transgender teen persona is where this week becomes operational. That student's ability to find safe resources runs directly through the vocabulary problem Drabinski and Bailund et al. describe: the words the teen uses may not be the words the catalog knows, and the gap is not an accident. Drabinski gives you a script for the reference encounter: lead the user to relevant material *and* name why their language is invisible in the system. Bailund et al. give you an institutional response beyond the encounter. Morales and Bourg's classification examples give you a pattern to look for with the undocumented family persona too: check what subject headings the library's materials on immigration status carry, and who they were written for.
- **Final essay:** "Arguing with the Algorithm" is nearly designed for this week. Drabinski's argument that a system's apparent objectivity is its most consequential fiction, and that exposing ruptures may serve users better than smoothing them, translates directly to recommender systems and search rankings. Bailund et al.'s finding that "neutrality" operates as an undefined, one-directional filter is the exact argument now made about content moderation and training-data curation. Pair either with a Block 4 reading and you have a thesis; add Winberry and Bishop's indirect/direct distinction and you have a way to evaluate whether any proposed remedy does anything.
- **Threads forward:** Week 9 scales the same structural analysis to global and economic inequality. Britz's information poverty is the classification problem at the level of whole societies. Block 4 asks whether the profession's commitments survive automation, and McEvoy's political environment is where those questions get decided. Block 5's capstone will ask what you now believe about neutrality; this is the week to start writing it down.
