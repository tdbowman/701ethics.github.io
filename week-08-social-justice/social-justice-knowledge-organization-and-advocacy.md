# Week 8: Social Justice, Knowledge Organization & Advocacy 🗂️

**Block 3: Diversity, Equity & Social Justice | October 12 – 18, 2026**

---

*A subject heading is a political decision wearing technical clothes.*

Last week you looked at who works in libraries and who is welcome in them. This week you look at the machinery: the classification schedules, subject vocabularies, collection decisions, and professional literatures through which libraries make claims about what the world contains and how it is organized. The argument running through all five readings is that this machinery is not a neutral container for knowledge but an active producer of it, and that its politics are more consequential precisely because they are invisible.

Begin with the distinction Morales, Knowles, and Bourg draw between *diversity* and *social justice*, because it organizes the week. Diversity describes the mix of individual characteristics in a group. Social justice "addresses power and privilege on a structural level, as well as at the level of mere representation." A perfectly diverse staff administering an unchanged system has achieved diversity and not justice. That is why the week's other readings are about systems rather than people.

### 🎯 What You'll Learn

- See how classification makes contingent judgments look inevitable
- Read the LCSH approval process from the inside, where neutrality is a decision criterion
- Distinguish diversity work from social justice work, and see why the difference bites
- Track what LIS research documents about social justice, and what it leaves out

### 🧠 Big Questions to Consider

- Morales, Knowles, and Bourg distinguish diversity from social justice. Which interventions in this week's readings are diversity work, which are justice work, and does the distinction hold up?
- Drabinski argues that correcting an offensive heading erases evidence of the catalog's ideology. Is a user's encounter with that evidence worth the harm of leaving the heading in place, and who bears each cost?
- Bailund and colleagues argue equity, not neutrality, is the right standard for judging heading proposals. What does a cataloger lose when neutrality is abandoned, and can that loss be defended?
- McEvoy predicts self-censorship rather than overt censorship as the dominant threat. How would you detect self-censorship in an institution, including your own?
