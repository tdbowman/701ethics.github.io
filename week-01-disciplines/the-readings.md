## 1. IFLA & UNESCO (2022)

**IFLA-UNESCO public library manifesto 2022**

> International Federation of Library Associations and Institutions & United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization. (2022). IFLA-UNESCO public library manifesto 2022. IFLA.

**Find it.** https://www.ifla.org/public-library-manifesto/ (English deposit copy: https://repository.ifla.org/items/0cd26664-4908-464c-8ade-f355c555730e)

**In the course project.** `IFLAUNESCOPublicLibraryManifesto.pdf`

Adopted 18 July 2022, the Manifesto argues from premises rather than evidence. It asserts that freedom, prosperity, and individual development are fundamental human values attainable only through well-informed citizens, and that democratic participation depends on "free and unlimited access to knowledge, thought, culture and information." From there it derives the public library as "the local gateway to knowledge" and "a basic condition for lifelong learning, independent decision-making and cultural development." Access is defended not as a service but as a precondition of democratic legitimacy. The same structure reappears in the IFLA Code of Ethics in Week 3.

The 2022 revision differs from its 1994 predecessor in ways worth marking. It frames libraries as components of "knowledge societies," names the Sustainable Development Goals, foregrounds digital delivery and media and information literacy, and grounds service design in "effective listening" to local need. It adds a strong clause on local and Indigenous knowledge requiring that communities "take an active role in identifying materials to be captured, preserved and shared, in accordance with the community's wishes." That quietly complicates the universalist framing: the document asserts both a global standard and a right of local self-determination over knowledge.

The operative sections are the ones readers skip. Access "shall in principle be free of charge"; libraries must be financed by national and local government; and copyright law "must ensure public libraries the same capacity to procure and give access to digital content on reasonable terms as is the case with physical resources," a direct intervention in licensing politics. Operations require a written policy built with community participation and long-term data collection to demonstrate impact to policy makers. There is no enforcement mechanism; the closing section merely urges implementation. The Manifesto's power is rhetorical and citational, and its language was engineered for exactly that use.

:::{tip} Why it's here
This is the only Week 1 text that states values as obligations rather than describing them as facts, and the only one written to be quoted by practitioners under pressure. It also establishes the global frame Objective O3 requires: the American documents in Weeks 2 and 3 look different once you have read the international one first.
:::

> "Collections and services should not be subject to any form of ideological, political or religious censorship, nor commercial pressures." (IFLA-UNESCO Public Library Manifesto 2022, "The Public Library," p. 2)

## 2. Bawden & Robinson (2022)

**The information science discipline**

> Bawden, D., & Robinson, L. (2022). The information science discipline. In Introduction to information science (2nd ed., chap. 1). Facet Publishing.

**Find it.** Through I-Share/ProQuest Ebook Central: https://i-share-dom.primo.exlibrisgroup.com/permalink/01CARLI_DOM/4v6i5v/cdi_askewsholts_vlebooks_9781783305261 (print ISBN 9781783304950; ebook 9781783305261). Chapter PDF in Perusall.

**In the course project.** `BawdenRobinson2022Introduction_to_Information_ScienceChapter1.pdf`

Bawden and Robinson concede at the outset that "information science" names at least four things: a computing and data science conception, an ICT conception, a physical and biological information conception, and a fourth "concerned with information recorded in documents, with meaning and knowledge," descended from librarianship and documentation. They adopt the fourth and say so. This is a positioned argument, not a neutral survey.

Their central move borrows from the philosopher of education Paul Hirst. Rather than a discipline with a unique form of knowledge (mathematics) or a practical discipline applying one form to problems (engineering), information science is a **field of study**: focused on a topic, free to draw on whichever forms of knowledge help. Bawden's earlier definition, quoted here, calls it "a multidisciplinary field of study, involving several forms of knowledge, given coherence by a focus on the central concept of meaningful recorded information." Two structuring devices follow: the **communication chain** of recorded information (creation, dissemination, collection, storage, organisation, description, retrieval, use, preservation) and Hjørland's **domain analysis** as the method for both research and practice.

Their claim to identity is about scope, not method. They acknowledge that journalists own creation, publishers dissemination, computer scientists retrieval, and concede that the field borrows most of its theory. Their answer to the "academic turf" problem is that "only the information sciences see their concern as being the totality" of the chain and the interactions among its parts. Whether that suffices they leave open, citing Furner's "Information science is neither" and Buckland's "What kind of science can information science be?" without resolution. The second half is institutional history: Schrettinger's *Bibliothekswissenschaft* in 1808, Otlet and La Fontaine's Institut International de Bibliographie in 1895, ASLIB in 1924, the American Documentation Institute becoming ASIS in 1968, Farradane coining "information science" and teaching it from 1961, and the iSchools movement since 2005. The chapter closes, via Shera and Vickery, with an argument that education should teach "theory, not techniques: principles, not practice." That is the rationale for a course like this one existing.

:::{tip} Why it's here
This is the only reading that gives you vocabulary for the field as a field: communication chain, domain analysis, field of study, the collection disciplines, GLAM. Everything else this semester is a case located somewhere on that chain. It also shows that the profession's self-definition has been unsettled for a century, which should make you skeptical when someone tells you what librarians "have always" believed.
:::

> "Information science is a field of study, with recorded information and documentation as its concern, focusing on the components of the information communication chain, studied through the perspective of domain analysis." (Bawden & Robinson, 2022, chap. 1, "The nature of information science")

## 3. Hepler & Horalek (2023)

**Introduction to library and information science**

> Hepler, R., & Horalek, D. (2023). Introduction to library and information science. College of Southern Idaho. (Chapters 1, 2, 3, and 17)

**Find it.** https://csi.pressbooks.pub/lis/ — Ch. 1 "Data, Information, and Knowledge": /chapter/knowledge-information-and-data/ · Ch. 2 "History of Libraries": /chapter/chapter-1/ · Ch. 3 "Library Services": /chapter/library-services/ · Ch. 17 "The Modern Library Experience": /chapter/the-library-experience/

This is an open textbook written for undergraduates, and reading it as a graduate student is itself an exercise. Take it seriously as an object: this is how the profession explains itself to newcomers, and its simplifications show what the profession treats as settled.

Chapter 1 builds a linear progression from data to information to knowledge. Data are "the bare facts of reality," quantitative or qualitative; information results when someone interprets and contextualizes data in a narrative; knowledge is what happens when a reader internalizes information. The chapter concedes that bias affects interpretation at every stage and uses that concession to justify information literacy instruction as a core responsibility. Compare deliberately with Bawden and Robinson, who treat the meaning of "information" as an open problem needing a chapter of its own.

Chapter 2 compresses history: cuneiform in Sumer around 2900 BCE, papyrus, the codex, Gutenberg in the 1450s, the great European collections, and a pivotal moment in the French Revolution when confiscated church and aristocratic libraries became state property and librarians Ameilhon and Van Praet processed hundreds of thousands of items, producing what the chapter calls "the democratic extension of library services to the general public." Then the American thread: the Library of Congress authorized in 1800, Jefferson's donation after the War of 1812, ALA founded October 6, 1876, Dewey, and OCLC. Note the implicit argument: public access is a political achievement of a specific revolutionary moment, not a timeless feature of libraries.

Chapter 3 is the operational map: patron services, interlibrary loan, reference and its four question types (directional, ready reference, specific search, research), technical services, digital services, and the academic/school/public/special typology. Chapter 17 argues that the modern library is a technology-integrated community hub while insisting that "the elitist foundations of the library still haunt all libraries today," and it criticizes diversity programming that quarantines minority representation into designated heritage months. That critique is your bridge to Block 3.

:::{tip} Why it's here
The other readings are written by and for people who already know what a circulation desk is. This one supplies the institutional furniture without which ethics talk stays abstract, and Chapter 17 plants the equity critique early so Block 3 does not feel bolted on.
:::

## 4. ALA (2026)

**The state of America's libraries: A snapshot of 2025**

> American Library Association. (2026). The state of America's libraries: A snapshot of 2025. American Library Association.

**Find it.** https://www.ala.org/news/state-americas-libraries-report-2026 (PDF: https://www.ala.org/sites/default/files/2026-04/state-of-americas-libraries-report-2026-WEB-acc-4.pdf)

The report is a set of short signed essays plus data, and its central analytical claim is that contemporary censorship is not a grassroots parental phenomenon. Sarah Lamdan, executive director of ALA's Office for Intellectual Freedom, reports attempts to censor **4,235 unique titles** in 2025, nearly 2,000 more than 2024 and five fewer than the 2023 record, against a baseline annual average of 273 between 2001 and 2020. Of those, 1,671 "represent the lived experience of LGBTQIA+ and BIPOC people."

The provenance data is the headline. Roughly 91.7% of titles challenged in 2025 were targeted by pressure groups (20.8%) and government decision makers (70.9%); 2.7% came from parents and 1.4% from individual users. Historically those organized actors accounted for about 12.9% of challenges, some 46 titles a year. In 2025 they targeted 7,884 books comprising 4,235 unique titles, meaning many titles were challenged repeatedly across jurisdictions; Lamdan reads that duplication as evidence of coordination. The report also tracks the legal landscape: the Fifth Circuit's May 2025 opinion in *Little v. Llano County*, described as contradicting established law affirming a First Amendment right to read in public libraries, and the Supreme Court's December 2025 refusal to hear it; removal laws in Florida, South Carolina, and Utah; a judge ordering Department of Defense schools to return nearly 600 books removed under an Executive Order; and on the other side new right-to-read laws in Connecticut, Delaware, and Rhode Island, rulings striking Florida's HB 1069 and Iowa's SF 496 as unconstitutionally vague, and voters in Ohio, Pennsylvania, and Texas rejecting censorship-focused board candidates.

Read the numbers carefully. These are documented attempts, self-reported to and compiled by an advocacy organization with an interest in the count, and rising figures partly reflect improved tracking. That does not make the finding wrong; it makes it one you must be able to defend methodologically when it is attacked.

:::{tip} Why it's here
This is your baseline for Weeks 2 and 3 and for the scenario. It converts "censorship" from a value word into a measured phenomenon with a documented structure, and hands you the most useful fact you will carry into the Censorship Crisis: the person at the microphone may be local, but the list they are reading from very likely is not.
:::

> "Approximately 91.7% of titles challenged in 2025 were targeted by pressure groups (20.8%) and government decision makers (70.9%). By comparison, only 2.7% of challenges came from parents, and 1.4% came from individual library users." (Lamdan, "Standing Together for the Freedom to Read," in ALA, *The State of America's Libraries: A Snapshot of 2025*, p. 6)
