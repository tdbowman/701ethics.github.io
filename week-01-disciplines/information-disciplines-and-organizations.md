# Week 1: Information Disciplines & Organizations 🌐

**Block 1: Foundations & Intellectual Freedom | August 24 – 30, 2026**

---

*Four texts, four incompatible answers to a question you cannot avoid: what profession is this?*

You are about to spend a semester arguing about the ethics of a profession, so start with an awkward question: what profession, exactly? These four texts give four different answers and they do not agree. Bawden and Robinson say you are joining a field of study organized around recorded information. The IFLA-UNESCO Manifesto says you are joining a global movement with a political mandate. Hepler and Horalek say you are learning concrete workplaces and workflows. The ALA's *State of America's Libraries* says you are entering an institution under organized attack. Hold all four; the friction between them is the week's real content.

Definitions have consequences. If information science is a field of study borrowing methods from sociology, philosophy, and computing, its ethics are borrowed too, and you will spend a career adapting other people's frameworks. If librarianship is instead a distinct practice with codified values, as the Manifesto and ALA insist, it has standing to make claims other institutions cannot. Notice that Bawden and Robinson stake disciplinary uniqueness on one move: other professions own pieces of the communication chain, but only the information sciences claim the whole of it. That is a modest claim. Ask whether it is strong enough to ground the immodest commitments you meet in Week 3.

### 🎯 What You'll Learn

- Map the field you are joining, and see that its own foundational texts do not agree on what it is
- Use the communication chain and domain analysis to locate any problem in the field
- Read the IFLA-UNESCO Manifesto as a document of obligations, not a description of reality
- Take the measure of the conditions you are entering, from the ALA's 2026 snapshot

### 🧠 Big Questions to Consider

- If information science is a "field of study" that borrows its methods from other disciplines, on what basis can it claim a distinctive professional ethics rather than importing one?
- The Manifesto forbids "ideological, political or religious censorship" of collections. Who enforces that sentence when the funder is the censor?
- Hepler and Horalek describe libraries through operations; Bawden and Robinson describe the field through concepts. Which is more useful in a hostile board meeting, and why?
