## 1. Padilla (2019)

**Responsible operations: Data science, machine learning, and AI in libraries (OCLC Research Position Paper)**

> Padilla, T. (2019). Responsible operations: Data science, machine learning, and AI in libraries (OCLC Research Position Paper). OCLC Research. https://doi.org/10.25333/xk7z-9g97

**Find it.** https://doi.org/10.25333/xk7z-9g97 (open access, CC BY 4.0)

**In the course project.** `PadillaOCLCResponsibleoperationsdatasciencemachinelearningai.pdf`

Commissioned by OCLC to produce a research agenda, Padilla produced something closer to a manifesto with a to-do list. The guiding principle is in the title: he adapts Chowdhury's concept of *responsible operations* to mean "individual, organizational, and community capacities to support responsible use of data science, machine learning, and AI." The word doing the work is *capacities*. Libraries already know algorithmic bias exists — the awareness gap has closed — but "significant gaps persist between concept and operationalization" at the level of workflows, policies, positions, collections, and infrastructure. Awareness is cheap; capacity is not.

Seven interdependent areas of investigation: (1) Committing to Responsible Operations; (2) Description and Discovery; (3) Shared Methods and Data; (4) Machine-Actionable Collections; (5) Workforce Development; (6) Data Science Services; (7) Sustaining Interprofessional and Interdisciplinary Collaboration. He is explicit that they do not decompose — description and machine-actionable collections "simply do not make sense" without workforce development and data science services, and none of it has a foundation without the first.

Four concepts inside that first area are worth carrying. *Managing bias* rather than eliminating it: from Nicole Coleman, the argument that elimination is impossible because it "would be a kind of bias itself," and that bias management belongs across collection development, description, instruction, and research support — with the blunt corollary that "monoculture cannot effectively manage bias," making staffing diversity a precondition. *Generous tools*: systems designed and documented so people of varying technical skill can contribute to or challenge algorithmic methods, so expertise elsewhere is not lost to a silo. *Distributed data science fluency*: the same argument at the level of people, so projects can be "critically championed—or challenged—by staff throughout an organization." And *constructive dissent*, quoted from Chowdhury: it is "self-defeating to create rules of ethical use without the institutional incentives and protections for workers engaged in these projects to speak up."

The workforce section is the one to read against Oladokun. Contributors wanted commitment to internal talent — mentoring, education, experiential opportunities, and clear paths to using what one learns "without the threat of it stacking onto existing job responsibilities" — and Padilla notes candidly that libraries may not compete on salary and should not comfort themselves that mission distinctiveness makes up the difference. On rights he calls assessment at scale a "wicked problem." He does not tell you whether to deploy; he tells you what your organization must be capable of before the question is meaningful, and he treats a decision *not* to use a technology as "a positive outcome for responsible operations."

:::{tip} Why it's here
It is the framing text for the whole block. Every later argument about chatbots, robots, or licensing is downstream of the claim that the binding constraint is organizational capacity, not technical capability — which lets you evaluate an AI proposal without pretending to evaluate the model.
:::

## 2. Oladokun et al. (2026)

**The rise of robots in libraries: Essential skills and knowledge for librarians in Industry 5.0**

> Oladokun, B. D., Maxwell, C. E., Umar, L., Alex-Nmecha, J. C., & Adigun, G. O. (2026). The rise of robots in libraries: Essential skills and knowledge for librarians in Industry 5.0. Information Services and Use, 46(3), 47–57. https://doi.org/10.1177/18758789261443981

**Find it.** https://doi.org/10.1177/18758789261443981 (online April 17, 2026; issue August 2026). Likely paywalled — check Canvas/Perusall for the licensed copy.

:::{warning} Verification note
NEW for Fall 2026 — replaces Tella (2020) and Asemi, Ko & Nowkarizi (2021). Verified: title, five authors, journal, volume 46, issue 3, pages 47–57, DOI, publication dates. STATUS: contents partially unverified — the full text was not read in preparing this chapter; the account below derives from the published abstract and metadata. Read the article before citing specifics.
:::

A PRISMA-based systematic review of 27 peer-reviewed studies published 2020–2025, asking what robotic technologies libraries are adopting and what competencies librarians need alongside them. Its framing device is Industry 5.0 — the proposition that the current moment is defined not by automation replacing humans but by human–machine collaboration, a deliberately different starting premise from the displacement narrative.

The review reports libraries deploying shelf-reading robots, automated storage and retrieval systems, humanoid service robots, chatbots, virtual assistants, and AI-driven intelligent systems. Note the range: this is a spectrum from invisible back-of-house logistics to an anthropomorphic machine standing where a person used to stand, and the ethical weight is not the same across it. The competencies identified combine technical and digital proficiency, AI literacy, ethical awareness, collaborative ability, and continuous professional development — a list that maps closely onto ACRL's four areas from Week 10, notable given that the two were produced independently and on different continents.

What most distinguishes this paper is unevenness. Preparedness varies significantly across contexts, shaped by funding, infrastructure, and institutional support; the barriers named are financial constraints, technical compatibility challenges, ethical concerns, and workforce anxieties. Put "workforce anxieties" beside "financial constraints" in one list and you have the week's argument in miniature: the anxiety is not irrational, because in an underfunded system automation is procured *because* it is cheaper than staff. The conclusion is programmatic rather than cautionary — integration requires curriculum reform, sustained investment, and clear policy frameworks supporting human–robot collaboration. Read that against Padilla's. Both end in institutional capacity; ask which an underfunded library can actually produce.

:::{tip} Why it's here
It is the most current picture of what "robots in libraries" concretely means, and the one Block 4 reading whose center of gravity is not North American or European. If your professional philosophy makes claims about the future of library work, it should survive contact with libraries whose binding constraint is not ethics review but electricity.
:::

## 3. Enis (2025)

**AI influencers: Libraries guiding AI use**

> Enis, M. (2025, September 16). AI influencers: Libraries guiding AI use. Library Journal. https://www.libraryjournal.com/story/ai-influencers-libraries-guiding-ai-use

**Find it.** https://www.libraryjournal.com/story/ai-influencers-libraries-guiding-ai-use

Enis reports that libraries are not merely subjects of the AI transition but can shape it — locally, through policy, procurement, community connections, and pedagogy — even though the technology's direction is set by a handful of enormous firms. He frames generative AI as an "arrival technology," a thing that is simply here and will restructure society. That framing is what makes librarian involvement urgent in his account, and it is the phrase a critical reader should flag as doing a great deal of unexamined work.

The concrete reporting is the value: the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill Libraries launching an AI Studio in Davis Library with workshops, speaker events, and hands-on instruction; Gwinnett County Public Library in Georgia offering courses on separating fact from fiction in generative AI; Austin Public Library building professional development to help city offices use AI ethically. He reports the claim that public libraries face what is characterized as the largest reskilling effort in human history, and that library workers hold real collective leverage through procurement decisions, community relationships, and expertise in information management and instruction.

Where to push: the piece argues for participation, and participation is how institutions acquire stakes. A library that has built an AI Studio, trained its city's staff, and marketed itself as the community's trusted AI guide is not neutrally positioned to later conclude the technology should be refused or scaled back. That is not a criticism of the three libraries named; it is Cox's point about ethical review of design decisions while the business decisions driving them go unreviewed, transposed into a library's own strategy.

:::{tip} Why it's here
The profession's arguments do not reach practitioners through peer-reviewed literature; they reach them through *Library Journal*. Reading trade press critically — noticing framing, sourcing, and the adjacency of vendor advertising — is a competency the scholarly readings will not teach you and the vendor negotiation will demand.
:::
