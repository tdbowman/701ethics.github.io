## 🗝️ Key Concepts

| Term | What it means |
|------|---------------|
| **Responsible operations** | individual, organizational, and community capacities supporting responsible use of data science, machine learning, and AI; Padilla's guiding principle, adapted from Rumman Chowdhury. |
| **Constructive dissent** | a governance culture where staff are empowered to object and protected when they do; rules of ethical use are self-defeating without it (Padilla, p. 8). |
| **Managing bias** | the alternative to debiasing, since elimination is impossible and would itself be a bias; requires diverse staffing because "monoculture cannot effectively manage bias" (p. 9). |
| **Generous tools** | tools designed so users of varying skill can contribute to, use, or challenge algorithmic methods, preventing siloed expertise (pp. 11–12). |
| **Distributed data science fluency** | enough methodological literacy across all roles that projects can be championed *or challenged* from anywhere (p. 11). |
| **Machine-actionable collections** | collections optimized for computational use in form, format, integrity, access method, and rights; tied to the CARE Principles and the Santa Barbara Statement. |
| **Training-data provenance** | recording where training data came from, which Padilla calls "a pervasive lack that compromises potential applications" (p. 10). |
| **Rights assessment at scale** | the "wicked problem" of clearing rights across millions of works; legal use does not equal ethical use (p. 17). |
| **Industry 5.0** | Oladokun et al.'s framing, in which the defining question is human–robot collaboration rather than replacement. |
| **Arrival technology** | Enis's framing of generative AI as already-present and society-reshaping; a move worth naming, because it converts a contestable adoption decision into an adaptation problem. |

All terms from all sixteen weeks are collected in the [Glossary](../glossary.md).

## 💬 Annotation & Discussion Prompts

These are written for **Perusall**. Each one points at something specific and each one is arguable, which is the point: annotation credit comes from advancing a conversation, not from summarizing a paragraph.

1. Padilla writes that a decision *not* to use a technology can be "a positive outcome for responsible operations." Annotate that claim and describe a realistic situation in which a director could say it aloud without ending their credibility. If you cannot construct one, say what that tells you about his agenda.
2. Enis reports libraries positioning themselves as trusted AI guides. Mark a passage on one named program and argue whether that library gained influence over the technology or acquired a stake in it. Use Cox's distinction between design and business decisions.
3. Oladokun et al. list "workforce anxieties" alongside "financial constraints" as barriers. Annotate the abstract and argue whether calling worker resistance a *barrier* is a neutral analytic choice or a managerial one. What would the same finding look like written from the workers' side?
4. **(Final-essay rehearsal)** Padilla demands provenance for training data and systems that let people iteratively correct a model. Apply both to the Block 5 integrator's report about you: what is its training data, what is its provenance, where exactly are you in its loop? Annotate the passage you rely on and state what you would *accept* as an adequate answer, not what you would like.
5. Padilla's generous tools and ACRL's competencies both assume non-specialists should be able to contest algorithmic systems. Annotate the generous tools section and describe what a *generous* assessment algorithm applied to students would look like. Is that possible, or does assessment require opacity to function?
6. ALA says libraries should not replace service roles with AI; Padilla says AI can free staff time to meet evolving demands. Annotate whichever you find weaker and reconstruct the strongest version of the position you disagree with.

## 🔗 Connections

- **Scenario:** This week arms the vendor role-play and cost-benefit branch. Padilla recommends evaluating the terms and conditions of cloud-based, off-the-shelf ML and AI solutions, asking specifically whether they breach privacy expectations and permit corporate data reuse beyond the original context. That recommendation is a script usable nearly verbatim at the table. His workforce section gives you the staffing counter to any efficiency case; Oladokun et al. give you concrete language for what "robotics" means when a vendor says it.
- **Final essay:** Padilla is the single most useful Block 4 reading for Part 1, because his concepts attack an assessment system at its joints rather than its outputs: provenance ("what was this trained on and can you show me?"), managing bias ("what bias has been declared rather than claimed as eliminated?"), generous tools ("can I contest this or only receive it?"), constructive dissent ("what happens to a student who objects?"). For Part 2, Enis and Oladokun et al. sharpen the actionable-commitments requirement: commitments about labor and procurement are testable in a way that commitments about "being critical" are not.
- **Threads forward:** Week 12 moves from the labor of running these systems to the labor embedded in their training data, and asks who owns what a machine learned from. It also moves human-in-the-loop into a space where the humans are patrons rather than staff.

## 📚 Going Deeper

Optional, and genuinely optional. These are also good places to find a final-essay angle.

- Tella, A. (2020). Robots are coming to the libraries: Are librarians ready to accommodate them? *Library Hi Tech News*. The prior anchor for this week and a useful earlier snapshot of professional readiness; in the project as `TaellaRobotsLibraries.pdf`. Verify volume/issue against the file before citing.
- Asemi, A., Ko, A., & Nowkarizi, M. (2021). Intelligent libraries: A review on expert systems, artificial intelligence, and robots. *Library Hi Tech*. Technical companion mapping expert systems and robotics applications; in the project as `AsemiIntelligentLibraries.pdf`. Verify volume/issue/pages against the file before citing.
- Reidsma, M. (2019). *Masked by trust: Bias in library discovery*. Library Juice Press. Book-length audit of bias in the discovery systems libraries already run; empirical grounding for the claim that library search is not neutral infrastructure.
- Crawford, K., & Joler, V. (2018). *Anatomy of an AI system*. https://anatomyof.ai/. The visual essay Cox relies on, tracing an Amazon Echo through mineral extraction, click labor, and energy; the clearest single artifact for the scenario's cost-benefit branch. ---
