# Week 9: Global Perspectives & Information Poverty 🌍

**Block 3: Diversity, Equity & Social Justice | October 19 – 25, 2026**

---

*Information poverty is a moral condition before it is a technical one.*

The block ends by widening the frame twice. First from the institution to the world: Weeks 7 and 8 examined exclusion inside a profession and inside its catalogs, and this week asks what equity means when the unit is a country, a development agenda, or a global economy. Second from access to capability: the readings converge on the claim that having information available is not the same as being able to use it, and that a profession organized around access alone has solved only part of its own problem.

Johannes Britz's 2004 article is the theoretical anchor, and it is worth asking why an article about information poverty appears in a professional ethics course rather than a development seminar. Britz's move is to insist information poverty is a *moral* concern and a matter of *justice*, not an infrastructure gap awaiting a technical fix. His definition is deliberately three-part: people lack the skills, abilities, *or* material means to access information, interpret it, and apply it — plus a lack of essential information and weak infrastructure. Each clause blocks a different easy answer. You cannot solve it by laying cable, because skills matter. You cannot solve it by training, because essential content may not exist in a usable language or form. You cannot solve it by publishing, because the resources the information refers to may not exist: as Britz notes, there is little reward in making information about medical services available to communities to whom those services cannot be provided. Note too that he tells you explicitly that information poverty is *not* reducible to the digital divide you met in Block 2 — it includes cultural and language diversity, education, and the ability to benefit, and it occurs between individuals sharing the same physical space. His illustration is a deliberate reversal worth sitting with: a well-educated New Yorker becomes information-poor in a remote African community.

### 🎯 What You'll Learn

- Define information poverty precisely enough to argue about it
- Work through Britz's account of what justice requires, and of whom
- Read the IFLA 2030 toolkit as advocacy infrastructure you could actually use
- Test global frameworks against one local library's real capacity

### 🧠 Big Questions to Consider

- Britz distinguishes information poverty from the digital divide. What does the distinction let you see that a connectivity framing does not, and what might connectivity catch that his framing does not?
- Britz's guideline 6 permits inequality in information distribution when it improves the situation of the information-poor. Is that a principled limit or a loophole large enough to justify most existing arrangements?
- The IFLA toolkit argues libraries should be written into national development plans. What does the profession give up, if anything, by framing its value in development terms?
- Ashiq and colleagues locate the main barriers in infrastructure and training rather than commitment. If that generalizes, what follows for the equity plans you will write in practice?
