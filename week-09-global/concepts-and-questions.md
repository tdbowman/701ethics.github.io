## 🗝️ Key Concepts

| Term | What it means |
|------|---------------|
| **Information poverty** | Britz: a situation in which individuals or communities lack the skills, abilities, or material means to access, interpret, and apply information, compounded by a lack of essential information and weak infrastructure. |
| **Essential information** | Britz's category for information required to survive and develop, which he argues must be treated as a public good and be non-exclusionary in most cases. |
| **Connectivity, content, and human approaches** | Britz's three families of explanation: missing technology, missing or unaffordable content, and missing capacity to benefit. |
| **Insiders and outsiders** | from Chatman via Britz: information poverty as a function of position within a community's shared knowledge base and symbols, which is why two people in the same room can differ in it. |
| **Self-alienation** | Britz's term for a community forced to operate within a foreign information context (language, technical idiom, imported infrastructure), producing dependence on the information-rich for both access and interpretation. |
| **Justice as recognition, reciprocity, participation, enablement, distribution, contribution** | Britz's six categories, each generating a different obligation toward the information-poor and a different remedy. |
| **Capability over goods** | Britz following Sen: fair distribution of opportunities and capabilities matters more than fair distribution of goods, because only the former enables self-development. |
| **The difference principle applied to information** | Britz's guideline 6, from Rawls: unequal distribution is permissible only where it does not disadvantage and in fact improves the position of the information-poor. |
| **Meaningful access** | the IFLA toolkit's operating term: space, staff, skills, and content together, as distinct from availability or connectivity alone. |
| **SDG Target 16.10** | the 2030 Agenda target on public access to information and fundamental freedoms, secured in part through the Lyon Declaration campaign, and the primary hook for library advocacy in national development planning. |

All terms from all sixteen weeks are collected in the [Glossary](../glossary.md).

## 💬 Annotation & Discussion Prompts

These are written for **Perusall**. Each one points at something specific and each one is arguable, which is the point: annotation credit comes from advancing a conversation, not from summarizing a paragraph.

1. Britz's guideline 6 permits inequality where it improves the situation of the information-poor. Annotate it with a real case (a pharmaceutical patent, a paywalled journal, a proprietary database) and argue whether it passes or fails his test. Be specific about who bears the cost.
2. Britz argues access without accessibility is empty, using a government publishing only online. Construct a counterargument: is there a threshold at which digital-only provision becomes defensible, and what would have to be true first?
3. The IFLA toolkit's case stories emphasize employment, agriculture, energy bills, and ICT skills. Annotate this pattern. What is systematically absent from an advocacy document written to persuade ministries, and what does the profession risk by making its case in these terms?
4. Britz's New Yorker example separates information poverty from wealth and education. Does the reversal clarify the concept or dilute it to the point where it can no longer identify injustice?
5. Ashiq and colleagues find the main barriers named by library directors are infrastructural rather than attitudinal. Annotate this against Hathcock in Week 7. Do the two accounts conflict, or describe different stages of the same problem?
6. Britz distinguishes six forms of justice. Take any one of his six practical guidelines, identify which form or forms it rests on, and argue whether it survives if that form is rejected.
7. The toolkit asks libraries to make their contribution legible within national development plans. Annotate this strategy against Britz's warning about paternalistic provision "without really determining the needs of the people." Can the toolkit's method meet Britz's condition?

## 🔗 Connections

- **Scenario:** "Building Inclusive Spaces" is due at the end of this week, Sunday, October 25. Britz keeps you from mistaking availability for access in the personas. The undocumented family may face no policy barrier at all and still be information-poor in his sense: the information exists, but the language, the interpretive context, the trust conditions, and, critically, the underlying services the information points to may not. His observation that "there is little reward in making information on medical services available to communities when these services cannot be provided to them" should shape what you conclude a library can and cannot fix. The Multicultural Library Manifesto tells you what the library owes; Britz tells you what the library alone cannot deliver, which is what separates a realistic action plan from a wish list.
- **Final essay:** "Arguing with the Algorithm" (6–8 pp., due Saturday, December 12) requires at least four readings from at least two blocks including Block 4. Britz is the strongest Block 3 candidate. His three-part definition of skills, abilities, and material means maps onto the current argument about algorithmic systems almost line for line: a system may be technically available to everyone and remain unusable by those without the interpretive capacity to contest its outputs, which is his human approach restated. His distinction between recognition, participation, and enablement lets you specify exactly which justice claim an algorithmic accountability proposal is making. Pair with the IFLA toolkit if your argument concerns policy and international governance; pair with Week 8's Drabinski or Bailund et al. if it concerns classification and the politics of neutrality.
- **Threads forward:** Block 4 takes the capability question into automated systems, where the gap between access and understanding is widest and the stakes of "meaningful access" are highest. Block 5's Ethics Journey capstone asks you to synthesize across the whole course; Britz's six forms of justice are the most portable analytic vocabulary this block gives you for that, because they let you say precisely what kind of wrong you think a practice commits.

## 📚 Going Deeper

Optional, and genuinely optional. These are also good places to find a final-essay angle.

- IFLA, Development and Access to Information (DA2I) (https://da2i.ifla.org/), IFLA's evidence platform tracking access to information against the SDGs; the data infrastructure the toolkit's advocacy method depends on, and a source for country-level evidence.
- The Lyon Declaration on Access to Information and Development (2014) (http://www.lyondeclaration.org/). The one-page campaign document that put access to information on the 2030 Agenda; short, and a useful study in how a profession writes for an international negotiation.
- Urban Libraries Council (https://www.urbanlibraries.org/). A North American public library leadership network with sustained programming on equity and libraries' role in local development; the domestic counterpart to the toolkit's international framing.
- Association for Rural & Small Libraries (https://www.arsl.org/), the corrective to an equity literature written largely from large urban and academic institutions; essential if you will work where the nearest branch is the only public infrastructure for many miles.
- Winberry, J., & Powell, G. (2026). Marginalized populations and institutions partnering to combat information marginalization: A case study of requited reclamative information practices. *Journal of Documentation, 82*(2), 407–426. https://doi.org/10.1108/JD-09-2025-0263 (a recent case study of institution–community partnership, useful as a direct-action counterpart to Britz's theory).
