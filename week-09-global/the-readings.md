## 1. IFLA (2021)

**Libraries, development and the United Nations 2030 Agenda (Revised version, March 2021)**

> International Federation of Library Associations and Institutions. (2021). Libraries, development and the United Nations 2030 Agenda (Revised version, March 2021). IFLA. (First published October 2015.)

**Find it.** https://www.ifla.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/assets/hq/topics/libraries-development/documents/libraries-un-2030-agenda-toolkit-2021.pdf

**In the course project.** `IFLAlibrariesun2030agendatoolkit2017.pdf`

The toolkit is an advocacy instrument in three parts: understanding the 2030 Agenda, engaging on the Sustainable Development Goals, and available tools. Its argument is that libraries are already doing development work and simply are not counted, and that the remedy is to make that contribution legible to the people writing national development plans. The substantive claim is that libraries supply "space, staff, skills and content to make a reality of meaningful access to information," and that meaningful access, not connectivity alone, is a precondition for progress across all seventeen goals. The pivot is Target 16.10, on public access to information and fundamental freedoms, which IFLA campaigned to include through the 2014 Lyon Declaration on Access to Information and Development. That history matters for how you read the document: it is written by an organization that got one line into an international agreement and is now trying to convert that line into national budget allocations.

The bulk of the toolkit is procedural, which is where it earns its place in an ethics course. It walks through eight advocacy actions: understand the implementation process in your country, coordinate your organization's work, mobilize the library field, gather evidence and stories, develop a communications strategy, build relationships with decision-makers, create advocacy partnerships, and evaluate whether the advocacy worked. It points to the specific venues where libraries can appear: Voluntary National Reviews, UN Country Teams, the High-Level Political Forum, Agenda 2063 in Africa, the Open Government Partnership. It also points to IFLA's Development and Access to Information (DA2I) reports as evidence infrastructure.

Its examples run to short case stories: agricultural information for farmers in Romania, employment information services, ICT programs for women and girls, energy-bill assistance, and the Marrakesh Treaty ratification campaign in Mongolia. Read these critically. They are chosen to persuade ministries, which means they emphasize measurable economic and service outcomes and say comparatively little about intellectual freedom, privacy, or the contested questions of Weeks 7 and 8. That is a strategic choice with costs, and identifying those costs is part of this week's work.

:::{tip} Why it's here
The toolkit is the primary global-policy document of the course, and central to Objective O4 as well as O3. It shows the profession operating as a political actor internationally and gives you an advocacy method that transfers to a city council or a provost. It also provides the contrast case for Britz: where Britz asks what justice requires, the toolkit asks what will persuade a ministry, and the gap between those questions is worth naming.
:::

## 2. Britz (2004)

**To know or not to know: A moral reflection on information poverty**

> Britz, J. J. (2004). To know or not to know: A moral reflection on information poverty. Journal of Information Science, 30(3), 192–204. https://doi.org/10.1177/0165551504044666

**Find it.** https://doi.org/10.1177/0165551504044666 (subscription; available through Dominican's library and posted in the project)

**In the course project.** `Britz.2004.ToKnow.pdf`

Britz builds in four moves: define the concept, identify its causes, establish it as a matter of justice, and derive practical guidelines. The definition synthesizes three approaches he finds in the literature. The *connectivity* approach locates information poverty in lack of access to ICTs: the OECD's digital divide framing, and Richard Heeks's observation that "new communication technologies are revolutionizing access to information – but the revolution is likely to reach everyone but the poor." The *content* approach locates it in the unavailability, unaffordability, and unsuitability of quality information itself. The *human* approach, hermeneutical rather than economic, holds that access alone is insufficient because people must be able to derive benefit, a capacity determined by skills, experience, and context; he cites UNESCO's preference for "knowledge societies" over "information societies" and Warschauer's argument that the divide is better understood as a literacy divide. His own definition folds all three together and adds infrastructure.

On causes, he identifies a fundamental layer (lack of essential information plus inability to assign meaning to it, distinguishing know-how, knowledge about attributes, and knowledge about knowledge), a socio-economic layer (globalization, and the commoditization of formerly collective information goods under intellectual property regimes such as TRIPS), an infrastructural layer, and a contextual layer. The contextual analysis is most original. Drawing on Chatman's insiders and outsiders, he argues two people in the same physical context can differ in information poverty because they differ in interpretive capacity, and offers the reversal of a New Yorker rendered information-poor in rural Africa. At community scale, ICT carries English and its own technical idiom, so it imposes a foreign information context that alienates communities from their own economic processes and forms of communication, producing dependence rather than capability.

The justice argument is where the article becomes an ethics text. Working from Rawls and from Aristotelian and Catholic social-teaching categories, he distinguishes six forms: justice as *recognition* (essential resources are owed to the information-poor because they are human), *reciprocity* (fair terms in agreements and exchanges), *participation* (equal opportunity rather than merely equal goods, following Sen on capabilities), *enablement* (reducing information dependence and powerlessness), *distribution* (fair distribution of essential information, including free or affordable provision), and *contribution* (the duty of knowledge creators toward the common good). He is explicit that he is not proposing to abolish markets in information; ethics plays "a normative role to ensure fairness in these processes."

The six closing guidelines are the most citable part. Guideline 1 asserts an equal right of access to essential information as fundamental and inalienable. Guideline 2 insists access implies both accessibility and the ability to benefit: if Botswana published all government information only online, most citizens could not exercise the right; and a red flag icon for new mail signals danger in South Africa. Guideline 3 calls for a minimum information standard set with the people who need it. Guideline 4 requires equal opportunity to exercise the right, potentially through subsidy, and warns against paternalistic provision that never asks what people need. Guideline 5 adopts the right to communicate, following Hamelink's point that "globalization without dialogue becomes homogenization and hegemony." Guideline 6 applies Rawls's difference principle: inequality in information distribution is permissible only where it does not disadvantage and in fact improves the situation of the information-poor.

:::{tip} Why it's here
Britz supplies the ethical theory the rest of the block uses informally. Weeks 7 and 8 asserted that equity is owed; Britz shows what kinds of arguments establish that, and distinguishes six of them precisely enough that you can tell which one you are making. His insistence that access is insufficient without capability is also the through-line back to Block 2's digital divide readings and forward to Block 4, where the question becomes whether people can meaningfully understand and contest automated decisions made about them.
:::

## 3. Ashiq et al. (2025)

**Reimagining academic libraries through a social justice lens: A phenomenological qualitative study of challenges and strategies in**

> Ashiq, M., Rehman, S. U., & Warraich, N. F. (2025). Reimagining academic libraries through a social justice lens: A phenomenological qualitative study of challenges and strategies in a developing country context. Information Development. Advance online publication, July 21, 2025. https://doi.org/10.1177/02666669251361022

**Find it.** https://doi.org/10.1177/02666669251361022 (subscription; available through Dominican's library)

This is an interview study, included precisely because it is empirical where the other two readings are theoretical and rhetorical. The authors conducted a phenomenological qualitative study with eighteen academic library directors in Pakistan, asking how equity, diversity, and inclusion principles can be strengthened in their institutions and what stands in the way.

Their reported findings identify gaps in four areas (inclusive services, accessible resources, infrastructure, and physical spaces), with shortfalls falling most heavily on students with disabilities and on marginalized populations more broadly. The framing matters: the barriers the directors describe are not primarily attitudinal or ideological but material and organizational, which makes them budgetary and political. The authors conclude that awareness-building, professional training, and capacity development for LIS professionals are the essential strategies for building genuinely equitable library environments.

Read this against Britz. His analysis predicts exactly this pattern: information poverty is co-determined by infrastructure and by the skills to benefit, and a library with neither accessible spaces nor trained staff fails on both axes his definition names. Read it also against the IFLA toolkit, whose case stories present libraries as capable development partners ready to deliver on the SDGs. Both pictures are accurate; they describe the same institutions from different distances. What the study adds to Block 3 is a reminder that the equity conversation in North American librarianship, with its emphasis on representation, vocabulary, and professional culture, is not the whole global conversation, and that a framework built entirely on the American case will not travel.

:::{tip} Why it's here
It is the block's only reading reporting what practitioners outside the North Atlantic say about equity work in their own institutions, which makes it the sharpest available check on Objective O3. It also supplies a comparative case for your action plan: the same equity commitment produces different first steps depending on what the institution already has.
:::
