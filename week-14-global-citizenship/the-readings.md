## 1. Floridi & Cowls (2019)

**A unified framework of five principles for AI in society**

> Floridi, L., & Cowls, J. (2019). A unified framework of five principles for AI in society. Harvard Data Science Review, 1(1). https://doi.org/10.1162/99608f92.8cd550d1

**Find it.** https://doi.org/10.1162/99608f92.8cd550d1 (open access, CC-BY 4.0)

**In the course project.** `Unified Framework of Five Principles.pdf`

The method is comparative and explicit. Six sets of principles are selected against three criteria: recent, relevant to AI's societal impact rather than to a single sector, and published by authoritative multi-stakeholder organizations of at least national scope. They are the Asilomar AI Principles (Future of Life Institute, 2017); the Montreal Declaration for Responsible AI (2017); the General Principles of the IEEE's *Ethically Aligned Design* v2 (2017); the Ethical Principles of the European Group on Ethics in Science and New Technologies (2018); the five overarching principles in the UK House of Lords report *AI in the UK: Ready, Willing and Able?* (2018); and the Tenets of the Partnership on AI. Together they yield 47 principles. The finding is high overlap, mapping onto the four principles of biomedical ethics associated with Beauchamp and Childress. The authors say the comparison should not surprise us, since of all areas of applied ethics bioethics most closely resembles digital ethics in dealing with new forms of agents, patients, and environments.

The five principles, precisely. **Beneficence**: promoting well-being, preserving dignity, and sustaining the planet; rendered as "well-being" in Montreal and IEEE, "common good" in AIUK and Asilomar, and in the EGE as both human dignity and sustainability, which the authors call the widest of all interpretations. **Non-maleficence** covers privacy, security, and capability caution, and is insisted upon as distinct from beneficence, since "do only good" and "do no harm" are not logically equivalent. Privacy appears as a principle in five of six sets. The memorable open question here is whether it is the developers or the technology being asked not to do harm. The authors put it as "whether it is Frankenstein or his monster against whose maleficence we should be guarding." **Autonomy**: the power to decide (to decide). Adopting AI means ceding some decision-making power to artefacts, so the principle requires balancing what we retain against what we delegate. The authors coin *meta-autonomy*, a decide-to-delegate model in which humans retain the power to decide which decisions to take, and any delegation remains overridable in principle. **Justice**, promoting prosperity, preserving solidarity, avoiding unfairness, is the principle whose meaning varies most, from eliminating discrimination (Montreal) to shared benefit and shared prosperity (Asilomar) to a defence of solidarity systems such as social insurance and healthcare (EGE, uniquely). **Explicability** is the addition, and the authors define it with precision: it incorporates the epistemological sense of intelligibility, answering "how does it work?", and the ethical sense of accountability, answering "who is responsible for the way it works?" The argument for it is functional. For AI to be beneficent and non-maleficent we must understand the good or harm it does; for AI to promote rather than constrain autonomy, our decision about who should decide must be informed by knowledge of how AI would act instead of us; for AI to be just, we must know whom to hold accountable when something goes wrong.

The paper closes by noting the framework's adoption by AI4People, then largely by the European Commission's High-Level Expert Group Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI, which in turn influenced the OECD Recommendation reaching 42 countries. That chain is the authors' evidence of movement from principles to practice.

:::{tip} Why it's here
Because these five words are the most portable critique vocabulary in the course, and because a framework built to solve principle proliferation is itself a fine object to interrogate. Part 1 of your essay needs terms for what the integrator does and does not do to you. "The report's dimension scores fail the explicability test in the intelligibility sense but partially satisfy it in the accountability sense, because the instructor has named himself as its author" is a sentence you can only write after this reading.
:::

> "The addition of the principle of 'explicability,' incorporating both the epistemological sense of 'intelligibility' (as an answer to the question 'how does it work?') and in the ethical sense of 'accountability' (as an answer to the question 'who is responsible for the way it works?'), is the crucial missing piece of the AI ethics jigsaw." (§3.5, p. 8)

## 2. Hanell et al. (2025)

**Canaries of democracy: The role of public libraries in times of political turmoil**

> Hanell, F., Carlsson, H., Engström, L., & Hansson, J. (2025). Canaries of democracy: The role of public libraries in times of political turmoil. Public Library Quarterly, 44(2), 198–222. https://doi.org/10.1080/01616846.2024.2385741

**Find it.** https://doi.org/10.1080/01616846.2024.2385741 (open access, CC BY 4.0, free at the DOI with no login)

The authors report the conclusions of a three-year Swedish project asking one question: how do librarians experience the library's legal mission to promote democracy when democratic norms are themselves under political attack? The setting matters. Sweden's Library Act of 2014 writes the democratic mission into statute, and Swedish cultural policy has run for fifty years on an arm's length principle that keeps politicians out of professional decisions. Over the last decade the Sweden Democrats, a radical right party, have become the second largest party in the country and have begun treating that arm's length principle as an obstacle. The title metaphor comes from coal mining. Libraries function as the bird in the tunnel, the institution that shows distress first when the political air turns bad.

The evidence is a longitudinal survey of library managers in all 77 municipalities of Sweden's six southernmost regions, run three times (June 2021, n=57; September 2022, timed to the general election, n=47; August 2023, n=48), with response rates between 62 and 74 percent. Questions covered three constant areas: the relationship between the library and local political decision-makers, the presence of library-related political discussion locally, and experiences of political pressure falling outside legitimate democratic channels. Focus groups and interviews from the same project are used to interpret the survey. The theory comes from Chantal Mouffe on antagonism and agonism, and from Ruth Wodak on the radical right's construction of fear, which lets the authors read the data as a conflict over who counts as "us."

The findings are more uncomfortable than the setup suggests. Across three years, more than 80 percent of managers said the relationship between politics and their library works well, and reported instances of illegitimate pressure stayed rare (8 respondents in 2021, 3 in 2022, 4 in 2023). What changes is the target. In 2021 the flashpoints were cultural and linguistic diversity, story times in Arabic, visiting authors from minority backgrounds. By 2023 every single respondent reporting pressure named programming, and the free text answers name Drag Story Hour and LGBTQI certification work. That shift tracks the national political discourse around the 2022 election exactly. Several managers also volunteered that political *disinterest* in libraries, rather than interference, is the democratic problem they feel daily, which the authors explain through the post-political condition of Swedish cultural policy: decades without real ideological argument left an opening the radical right walked into.

The most useful finding for an ethics course is self-censorship. The interview data show libraries dropping or never proposing programming out of fear of what might happen, triggered by rumors about a neighboring municipality or a general sense of an unfriendly local climate, with no actual threat received. Fear works twice here: as the radical right's rhetoric about "the other," and as librarians' own apprehension of confrontation. The authors close by arguing that a professional self-understanding built on neutrality actively disables librarians, because it lets them treat advocating for democracy or hosting agonistic disagreement as somebody else's job. They place this in the post-neutrality librarianship debate and note that managers' worries have scaled up from local budgets to the Library Act itself.

:::{tip} Why it's here
Floridi and Cowls give you the vocabulary at altitude: five principles distilled from six sets of AI guidelines, with a warning that piling up more principle documents produces confusion and lets organizations shop for the ethics they prefer. That paper never touches an institution. This one is the institution. It shows what happens to a library when its ethical commitments, access for everyone and particular attention to national minorities and to people whose first language is not the national language, collide with a political majority that disputes them, and it shows the collision producing quiet pre-emptive withdrawal rather than dramatic censorship. It also gets you out of the American frame. Sweden has a statutory democratic mission for libraries that the United States has no equivalent of, and even with that statute the same pressures land on the same programming and the same communities. That comparison is the point of the week.
:::

> "In most cases, this is not a response to actual threats or attempts of illegitimate political influence but acts out of fear of what *might* happen if controversial programming activities are arranged, such as story times in Arabic or celebrations of Pride week." (Discussion section)
