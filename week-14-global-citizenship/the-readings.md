## 1. Floridi & Cowls (2019)

**A unified framework of five principles for AI in society**

> Floridi, L., & Cowls, J. (2019). A unified framework of five principles for AI in society. Harvard Data Science Review, 1(1). https://doi.org/10.1162/99608f92.8cd550d1

**Find it.** https://doi.org/10.1162/99608f92.8cd550d1 (open access, CC-BY 4.0)

**In the course project.** `Unified Framework of Five Principles.pdf`

The method is comparative and explicit. Six sets of principles are selected against three criteria — recent, relevant to AI's societal impact rather than to a single sector, and published by authoritative multi-stakeholder organizations of at least national scope. They are the Asilomar AI Principles (Future of Life Institute, 2017); the Montreal Declaration for Responsible AI (2017); the General Principles of the IEEE's *Ethically Aligned Design* v2 (2017); the Ethical Principles of the European Group on Ethics in Science and New Technologies (2018); the five overarching principles in the UK House of Lords report *AI in the UK: Ready, Willing and Able?* (2018); and the Tenets of the Partnership on AI. Together they yield 47 principles. The finding is high overlap, mapping onto the four principles of biomedical ethics associated with Beauchamp and Childress — a comparison the authors say should not surprise us, since of all areas of applied ethics bioethics most closely resembles digital ethics in dealing with new forms of agents, patients, and environments.

The five principles, precisely. **Beneficence** — promoting well-being, preserving dignity, and sustaining the planet; rendered as "well-being" in Montreal and IEEE, "common good" in AIUK and Asilomar, and in the EGE as both human dignity and sustainability, which the authors call the widest of all interpretations. **Non-maleficence** — privacy, security, and capability caution; insisted upon as distinct from beneficence, since "do only good" and "do no harm" are not logically equivalent. Privacy appears as a principle in five of six sets. The memorable open question here is whether it is the developers or the technology being asked not to do harm — "whether it is Frankenstein or his monster against whose maleficence we should be guarding." **Autonomy** — the power to decide (to decide). Adopting AI means ceding some decision-making power to artefacts, so the principle requires balancing what we retain against what we delegate. The authors coin *meta-autonomy*, a decide-to-delegate model in which humans retain the power to decide which decisions to take, and any delegation remains overridable in principle. **Justice** — promoting prosperity, preserving solidarity, avoiding unfairness; the principle whose meaning varies most, from eliminating discrimination (Montreal) to shared benefit and shared prosperity (Asilomar) to a defence of solidarity systems such as social insurance and healthcare (EGE, uniquely). **Explicability** — the addition, defined with precision: it incorporates the epistemological sense of intelligibility, answering "how does it work?", and the ethical sense of accountability, answering "who is responsible for the way it works?" The argument for it is functional. For AI to be beneficent and non-maleficent we must understand the good or harm it does; for AI to promote rather than constrain autonomy, our decision about who should decide must be informed by knowledge of how AI would act instead of us; for AI to be just, we must know whom to hold accountable when something goes wrong.

The paper closes by noting the framework's adoption by AI4People, then largely by the European Commission's High-Level Expert Group Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI, which in turn influenced the OECD Recommendation reaching 42 countries — the authors' evidence of movement from principles to practice.

:::{tip} Why it's here
Because these five words are the most portable critique vocabulary in the course, and because a framework built to solve principle proliferation is itself a fine object to interrogate. Part 1 of your essay needs terms for what the integrator does and does not do to you. "The report's dimension scores fail the explicability test in the intelligibility sense but partially satisfy it in the accountability sense, because the instructor has named himself as its author" is a sentence you can only write after this reading.
:::

> "The addition of the principle of 'explicability,' incorporating both the epistemological sense of 'intelligibility' (as an answer to the question 'how does it work?') and in the ethical sense of 'accountability' (as an answer to the question 'who is responsible for the way it works?'), is the crucial missing piece of the AI ethics jigsaw." (§3.5, p. 8)

## 2. Hanell et al. (2025)

**Canaries of democracy: The role of public libraries in times of political turmoil**

> Hanell, F., Carlsson, H., Engström, L., & Hansson, J. (2025). Canaries of democracy: The role of public libraries in times of political turmoil. Public Library Quarterly, 44(2), 198–222. https://doi.org/10.1080/01616846.2024.2385741

**Find it.** https://doi.org/10.1080/01616846.2024.2385741 — check Perusall/Canvas; repository record at https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/publication/66a2e5c6-c35c-4473-a8b6-a80cd6b5b05e

Because I have not read the full text, this describes what the article is and what it is positioned to do, and stops there. Do not attribute findings to it on my authority.

It comes out of a sustained Swedish research program on public libraries in a changed political landscape, involving researchers at Linnaeus University and Lund University. That program's 2024–2025 output addresses a connected set of questions: how the consensus model in Swedish cultural policy came apart and what the public library became once it did; how drag story hour events became a site for the construction of fear and othering in Swedish policy debate; how librarians in Sweden and Denmark negotiate the boundaries of professional practice under political pressure; and how far-right actors frame museums and public libraries in online discourse. "Canaries of democracy" is the synthesizing piece, and the metaphor carries the claim: public libraries register democratic distress early, before it is visible elsewhere, which makes what happens to them diagnostic rather than parochial.

The significance for us is comparative. Nearly everything else you have read about pressure on libraries in this course has been American. This gives you a second national context with a very different welfare-state and cultural-policy history, in which analogous pressures are appearing. If the phenomenon reproduces across systems that different, explanations resting on American particularities are incomplete. Read with two questions in hand. What is the unit of the argument — the individual librarian, the institution, the profession, or the state? And does "canary" grant libraries a role they want? A canary is valuable because it dies first. That is a striking thing to say about a profession.

:::{tip} Why it's here
Because "global digital citizenship" is an empty phrase unless you have looked at how the citizenship part is going somewhere other than here. It also keeps the block from ending in pure technology talk: the threat to information access in 2026 is not only algorithmic.
:::
