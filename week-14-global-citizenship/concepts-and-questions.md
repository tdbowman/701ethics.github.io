## 🗝️ Key Concepts

| Term | What it means |
|------|---------------|
| **Beneficence** | promoting well-being, preserving dignity, and sustaining the planet; the most widely shared of the five principles across the six documents (Floridi & Cowls, §3.1). |
| **Non-maleficence** | preventing harm, centrally through privacy protection, security, and capability caution; logically distinct from beneficence (§3.2). |
| **Autonomy** | the balance between decision-making power humans retain and power delegated to artificial agents, the risk being that growth in artificial autonomy undermines human flourishing (§3.3). |
| **Meta-autonomy / decide-to-delegate** | the model in which humans retain the power to decide *which* decisions to take, with any delegation overridable in principle (§3.3). |
| **Justice** | promoting prosperity, preserving solidarity, avoiding unfairness; the principle whose meaning diverges most across the six documents (§3.4). |
| **Explicability** | intelligibility ("how does it work?") plus accountability ("who is responsible for the way it works?"); added because it enables the other four (§3.5). |
| **Principle proliferation** | so many published sets of AI principles that they become either redundant or contradictory, inviting stakeholders to shop for the least demanding set (§1). |
| **Market for principles** | the failure mode in which organizations select among ethical frameworks for convenience rather than merit (§1, citing Floridi 2019b). |
| **Canary metaphor** | Hanell et al.'s framing of public libraries as early indicators of democratic strain, registering political distress before other institutions (2025). |
| **Post-consensus cultural policy** | the condition in which the shared political agreement insulating libraries from partisan contest has broken down (Hanell, Carlsson, & Engström, 2024, and related work). |

All terms from all sixteen weeks are collected in the [Glossary](../glossary.md).

## 💬 Annotation & Discussion Prompts

These are written for **Perusall**. Each one points at something specific and each one is arguable, which is the point: annotation credit comes from advancing a conversation, not from summarizing a paragraph.

1. Floridi and Cowls claim all 47 principles from six documents are captured by their five (Table 1). Identify one ethical concern from Blocks 1–4 that you think the five do *not* capture. Argue for a sixth principle or explain why yours folds into an existing one.
2. Mark the Frankenstein passage (§3.2). Whose maleficence should a library guard against when it licenses a discovery layer with an AI summarization feature: the vendor's, the model's, or its own?
3. Annotate the meta-autonomy passage (§3.3) and apply it to your own use of generative AI in this course: which decisions did you delegate, and was that delegation overridable in practice or only in principle?
4. In §4 the authors say the framework needs under-represented perspectives and, in the same section, that "Ethics is not the preserve of a single continent or culture." Argue whether those are consistent.
5. The authors treat adoption by AI4People, the EU High-Level Expert Group, and the OECD as evidence of impact. Argue the opposite reading: that convergence among institutions with overlapping membership is evidence of a closed conversation.
6. Annotate the "canary" metaphor in Hanell et al. Does it strengthen the case for protecting libraries, or accept a role — dying first as a public service — that no profession should accept?
7. Compare UNESCO's *fairness and non-discrimination* principle with Floridi and Cowls's *justice*. Where do they diverge most, and which is more usable by a working librarian?

## 🔗 Connections

- **Scenario:** These five principles are the instrument you will use on your own capstone report. Before writing, run the report through all five. Does it promote your well-being as a learner (beneficence)? Could it harm you — by mislabeling you, by producing a record, by shaping how you see yourself (non-maleficence)? Does it preserve your authority over your own professional identity (autonomy)? Does it treat all students equitably given that scenario performances allowed multiple attempts (justice)? Can you tell how it works, and do you know who is answerable for it (explicability)? At least one will produce a paragraph worth keeping.
- **Final essay:** Week 14 is your critique vocabulary for Part 1. The most useful available move is to split explicability in two and evaluate the integrator on each half independently — the instructor has been explicit that the scores derive from his own reading of the course materials, which is an unusually strong accountability claim and an unusually weak intelligibility claim. That asymmetry is an argument, not just an observation.
- **Threads back:** This closes the loop on Block 2. The privacy and digital divide readings gave you harms; the five principles give you the categories those harms fall into, and Hanell gives you the political conditions under which they get addressed or ignored.
