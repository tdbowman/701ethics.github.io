## 1. ALA (2025)

**Equity, diversity, inclusion, and belonging: An interpretation of the Library Bill of Rights**

> American Library Association. (2025). Equity, diversity, inclusion, and belonging: An interpretation of the Library Bill of Rights. Adopted June 27, 2017, under the previous name "Equity, Diversity, Inclusion"; amended May 29, 2025.

**Find it.** https://www.ala.org/advocacy/intfreedom/librarybill/interpretations/EDI

**In the course project.** `ALAEquity Diversity Inclusion and Belonging_ An Interpretation of the Library Bill of Rights.pdf`

The interpretation makes a structural argument before a moral one. Rather than treating equity as an obligation added to intellectual freedom, it asserts that equity, diversity, inclusion, and belonging are "central to promoting and practicing intellectual freedom," then walks through the seven articles of the Library Bill of Rights, reading each as an equity commitment. Article I's protection against excluding materials because of the "origin, age, background, or views" of their creators becomes a mandate for actively acquiring underrepresented voices; Article V's guarantee of the right to use a library becomes an audit of exclusionary practices. Its four terms are defined with care: equity "means considering differences to ensure a fair process and outcome" and names historical barriers that "still exist today"; diversity is the sum of sameness and difference; inclusion is an environment; belonging is the felt experience. Alongside these runs a staffing claim: library workers, administrators, and governing bodies "should reflect the origins, age, background, and views of their community."

The operationally useful passages are the concrete ones. On collections: mainstream publishers "have historically underrepresented diverse authors and viewpoints," so libraries should pursue alternative, small-press, independent, and self-published content. Its insistence that interlibrary loan "should not replace building local diverse local collections" takes a direct shot at a common cost-saving evasion. On access: remove identification requirements, overdue charges and fees, and deposits for service, and base restrictions solely on behavior. On privacy: "surveillance and monitoring harms marginalized groups the most," making privacy an equity issue rather than a separate value, and bridging back to Block 2.

Where it lands is a deliberate double commitment some readers find principled and others incoherent. The document demanding active inclusion of marginalized voices also insists meeting rooms and exhibits remain open to "content and groups that some community members may find objectionable or offensive," and that "controversy alone should not dictate policy." ALA's position is that these are one commitment seen from two angles. Whether it holds under pressure is being actively litigated.

:::{tip} Why it's here
This is the profession's own authoritative statement of what Block 3 is about, and the baseline against which every critique in Weeks 7 and 8 is measured. Read it first so that when Hathcock says diversity initiatives "recruit for whiteness," you know precisely which commitments she is holding to account, and can tell a critique of the values from a critique of their implementation.
:::

> "Belonging is when every person feels truly welcomed and included." (Section defining equity, diversity, inclusion, and belonging)

## 2. IFLA & UNESCO (2012)

**IFLA/UNESCO multicultural library manifesto: The multicultural library — a gateway to a cultural diverse society in dialogue**

> International Federation of Library Associations and Institutions & UNESCO. (2012). IFLA/UNESCO multicultural library manifesto: The multicultural library — a gateway to a cultural diverse society in dialogue. IFLA. (Approved by the IFLA Governing Board August 2006; adopted by UNESCO at its 35th Session, October 2009; this printing March 2012.)

**Find it.** https://www.ifla.org/ifla-unesco-multicultural-library-manifesto/

**In the course project.** `multicultural_library_manifestoen.pdf`

The Manifesto opens from demography rather than rights. There are more than 6,000 languages; migration is rising; the result is "an increasing number of people with complex identities." Cultural diversity is not a challenge arriving at the library's door; it is the condition of the society the library is already in. Borrowing UNESCO's 2001 definition, it treats culture expansively, as "lifestyles, ways of living together, value systems, traditions and beliefs" rather than art and literature alone.

Its principles are short and demanding: serve all community members without discrimination based on cultural and linguistic heritage; provide information in appropriate languages and scripts; give access to materials reflecting all communities; and (the one most often quietly dropped) "employ staff to reflect the diversity of the community, who are trained to work with and serve diverse communities." It names the populations most likely to fall through: minorities, asylum seekers and refugees, residents on temporary permits, migrant workers, and Indigenous communities.

Its sharpest move is administrative. Under "Management and operation," multicultural services are core activities, not separate or additional, and libraries need a written policy and strategic plan grounded in a comprehensive user-needs analysis with resources attached. This is a defense against the pattern where multicultural service exists as a grant-funded pilot, a single position, or a shelf, and vanishes when the grant ends. It also asks for preservation of oral, Indigenous, and intangible heritage; multilingual outreach; access systems in appropriate languages; and continuing education in cross-cultural communication and anti-discrimination. It closes by pushing responsibility upward, to governments "urged to establish and adequately fund" such services, and outward, to international networks and to research.

:::{tip} Why it's here
It is the global counterpart to the ALA interpretation and the primary site in this course for Objective O3. It also supplies what the ALA document does not: a management framework. When you build an equity plan, the Manifesto gives you the structure (policy, needs analysis, resourcing, staffing, evaluation) that turns a commitment into a service.
:::

> "The core activities of library and information services for culturally and linguistically diverse communities are central, not 'separate' or 'additional', and should always be designed to meet local or specific needs." (Section: Management and operation)

## 3. Hathcock (2015)

**White librarianship in blackface: Diversity initiatives in LIS**

> Hathcock, A. (2015, October 7). White librarianship in blackface: Diversity initiatives in LIS. In the Library with the Lead Pipe.

**Find it.** https://www.inthelibrarywiththeleadpipe.org/2015/lis-diversity/ (open access, peer reviewed)

**In the course project.** `WhiteLibrarianshipInBlackface.pdf`

Hathcock begins from an observation rather than a thesis: LIS runs many diversity initiatives, among them ARL and SAA's Mosaic Program, AALL's George A. Strait Minority Scholarship, and ALA's Spectrum Scholars. The demographics have barely moved. Quoting Jennifer Vinopal, she names the mechanism: "We're bringing [people] from underrepresented identity groups into the profession at same rate they're leaving. Attrition [is] a problem." Recruitment without retention is a treadmill.

Her explanation turns on a specific definition of *whiteness*, drawn from Espinal, Honma, Hall, Galvan, and Bourg: not a description of skin but "an ideological practice," a system of exclusion based on hegemony that, in Galvan's compressed formulation, "means: white, heterosexual, capitalist, and middle class." Its power lies in invisibility. Unnamed, it operates as the neutral background against which everyone else registers as different. Her examples are concrete: a librarian of color mistaken for a library assistant at a conference; a genderqueer librarian forced into a binary restroom choice; a working-class librarian told to "just take a job anywhere and move" by colleagues who assume the money exists.

The article's original contribution is reading application requirements as a selection mechanism. The ARL Career Enhancement Program asks for six things: a form, a résumé, a 500-word essay, a letter of acceptance to an ALA-accredited program, official transcripts, and two letters of recommendation with one from a professor or employer. She shows each requirement quietly presuming temporal, financial, and educational privilege. A working-class applicant may have extensive low-wage experience but no framework for translating it into transferable skills. A genderqueer applicant who has changed name and gender markers faces an identity-verification labor no cisgender applicant performs. The result is a chilling effect and self-selection: those who apply and succeed are already fluent in the norms the program was meant to widen.

Her remedy has two halves, and she is explicit that the first does not require lowering standards. Redesign the programs: accept, for instance, recommendation letters from community members who genuinely know an applicant's work. And meanwhile, mentor: teach new librarians both to navigate the existing system and to dismantle it, in the spirit of the National Association of Colored Women's motto, "Lifting as We Climb." She names #critlib, #radlib, ALA caucuses, and ACRL's Dr. E. J. Josey Spectrum Scholar Mentor Program as existing infrastructure, and is candid about her own position: a middle-class Black woman raised to "play the game and do what you want later," and a 2012 Spectrum Scholar.

:::{tip} Why it's here
Hathcock is the pivot of the week and probably of the block. It converts diversity from a headcount problem into a structural one, with a mechanism specific enough to test. It also supplies the analytic move that Mejia and Chiu, Drabinski, and Bailund et al. each perform on a different object: look past the stated goal of a professional practice to the norms encoded in its procedures.
:::

> "Our diversity programs do not work because they are themselves coded to promote whiteness as the norm in the profession and unduly burden those individuals they are most intended to help." (Section: Failure of Diversity Initiatives in LIS)

## 4. ALA COPE (2026)

**ALA Code of Ethics 9th principle: COPE ableism Q&A**

> American Library Association, Committee on Professional Ethics. (2026). ALA Code of Ethics 9th principle: COPE ableism Q&A. Approved June 30, 2026.

**Find it.** https://www.ala.org/tools/ethics/ableism

This is a Q&A, not an interpretation, produced by ALA's Committee on Professional Ethics to explain the ninth principle of the Code of Ethics, the principle added in June 2021 committing library workers to dismantle systemic and individual biases, confront inequity and oppression, and advance racial and social justice through hiring, resource allocation, spaces, and services. Its occasion is an omission it names directly: ableism is not explicitly written into the ninth principle, and the Q&A exists to argue it is nonetheless squarely within scope.

Its structure is roughly ten practical questions: what a disability is, what ableism is, how human and legal rights operate in libraries, what affects disabled patrons and disabled staff, how ableism relates to library ethics, how to train staff, what policies to adopt, which pandemic-era access practices to retain, what universal design means in a library, and what leadership owes. That last cluster matters: accessibility is treated as a leadership and budget question, not a front-line improvisation.

Conceptually, its decisive move is from a medical model of disability to a social one. Under a medical model, disability is a deficit located in a person and addressed by accommodation on request. Under the social model the Q&A adopts, the barrier is in the environment and the obligation runs to the institution in advance of any request. It frames this as a majority-adjacent design problem rather than an edge case by citing prevalence figures, roughly 15% of the global population living with at least one disability (WHO) and 27% of the U.S. population (CDC). Its recommendations follow: universal design, disability-inclusive policy, staff training, and deliberate resource allocation.

:::{tip} Why it's here
The newest reading in Block 3, the Q&A fills the gap most visible in the older documents: the ALA interpretation names disability once, in a list of prohibited grounds; the Multicultural Library Manifesto does not address it. Reading a 2026 ethics document against a 2012 manifesto and a 2015 critique lets you watch the profession's understanding of "diversity" expand in real time, and ask which axes the current consensus still leaves out. It is direct preparation for the invisible-disability persona in this block's scenario.
:::

> "Accessibility and inclusion are not the responsibility of people who are disabled, but rather, it is the responsibility of an organization to anticipate and meet the needs of all." (Definitional section on disability and ableism)

## 5. Mejia & Chiu (2025)

**Interest convergence, intersectionality, and counter-storytelling: Critical race theory as practice in scholarly communications li**

> Mejia, M., & Chiu, A. (2025, August 20). Interest convergence, intersectionality, and counter-storytelling: Critical race theory as practice in scholarly communications librarianship. In the Library with the Lead Pipe.

**Find it.** https://www.inthelibrarywiththeleadpipe.org/2025/interest-convergence/ (open access, peer reviewed)

Mejia and Chiu take Hathcock's diagnosis as settled and ask a practitioner's follow-up: given that DEI rhetoric has not shifted racial power in librarianship, what does a BIPOC library worker actually do on Monday morning? Their answer is that critical race theory is not only explanatory but usable, and they work three of its concepts through two domains: collegial relationships and scholarly communications work.

*Interest convergence*, from Derrick Bell's reading of *Brown v. Board of Education*, holds that racial progress occurs when the interests of BIPOC and white people converge. They treat it as strategically useful and morally limited: it explains why equity proposals succeed when framed as institutional wins, and why such gains are fragile, since a convergence can end. *Intersectionality*, from Kimberlé Crenshaw and Patricia Hill Collins, they use to recognize "many ways to embody multiply-marginalized identities at work" and to build flexibility into scheduling and workload. *Counter-storytelling* they define as telling stories reflecting marginalized experience in ways that challenge dominant narratives, and use to surface how white supremacy operates in ordinary practice rather than overt hostility.

The applications are specific. In collegial life: creating counter-spaces where BIPOC colleagues regroup; practicing selective *acceptance* of service requests rather than selective refusal; naming emotional and otherwise invisibilized labor explicitly in promotion materials. In scholarly communications teaching: treating citation as political rather than mechanical and pointing researchers toward the Cite Black Women Collective; teaching copyright as a tool for widening access, including exercises in which researchers imagine negotiating to keep their copyright; and holding a critical line on open access, noting that "traditional publishers have co-opted the open access publishing model for their own profit-making goals by charging exorbitant fees." Their conclusion turns the profession's question around: rather than asking how to hire more BIPOC library workers, ask why the profession remains so white after decades of DEI work.

:::{tip} Why it's here
It updates Hathcock by ten years and moves the analysis from recruitment into daily practice, where you will spend your career. It also introduces intersectionality and counter-storytelling as working concepts, both of which you need for the perspective-taking scenario, where the entire pedagogical device is a counter-story and where several personas sit at intersections rather than on a single axis.
:::

> "Critical race theory (CRT) offers the tools necessary to understand why the underlying racial power dynamics of our profession remain unchanged and to generate new ideas to move toward true equity and inclusion." (In Brief)
