## 🗝️ Key Concepts

| Term | What it means |
|------|---------------|
| **Belonging** | the felt experience of being genuinely welcomed; distinguished in the 2025 ALA interpretation from equity, diversity, and inclusion because it names a state in a person rather than an action by an institution. |
| **Whiteness as normative** | in Hathcock, an ideological practice rather than a racial description: the unnamed standard against which everything else registers as different, extending across race, gender identity, sexuality, and class. |
| **Playing at whiteness** | Hathcock's term for the performance of dominant professional norms that underrepresented practitioners must master to enter and remain in the field, and which diversity applications implicitly test for. |
| **Recruiting for whiteness** | Hathcock's mechanism: requirements (résumés, official transcripts, faculty recommendations) that presume the time, money, and relationships exclusion has withheld, thereby selecting for assimilability. |
| **Multicultural services as core, not additional** | the IFLA/UNESCO principle that services to linguistically and culturally diverse communities belong in the base budget and strategic plan, not a discretionary special-projects line. |
| **Social model of disability** | from the ALA Ableism Q&A: locating disability in environmental and institutional barriers rather than individual bodies, which relocates the duty to remove them onto the organization. |
| **Universal design** | designing spaces, systems, and services usable by the widest range of people from the outset, rather than retrofitting accommodations on individual request. |
| **Interest convergence** | from Bell via Mejia and Chiu: racial progress occurs where BIPOC and white interests align, which makes such progress both achievable and revocable. |
| **Intersectionality** | from Crenshaw and Collins: marginalizations compound rather than stack, so holding multiple marginalized identities is not the sum of its parts. |
| **Counter-storytelling** | telling stories grounded in marginalized experience to contest dominant professional narratives; both a CRT method and, this block, a pedagogical form. |

All terms from all sixteen weeks are collected in the [Glossary](../glossary.md).

## 💬 Annotation & Discussion Prompts

These are written for **Perusall**. Each one points at something specific and each one is arguable, which is the point: annotation credit comes from advancing a conversation, not from summarizing a paragraph.

1. The ALA interpretation states that full EDIB commitment "requires that library collections and programming represent many kinds of ideas, stories, and experiences—even those that some find controversial," and separately requires meeting rooms open to groups some patrons find "objectionable or offensive." Annotate both passages together: one principle or two in conflict? Argue a position and say what would change your mind.
2. Hathcock walks through six ARL application requirements. Pick the one you find *least* obviously exclusionary and make the strongest case for retaining it as written. Then answer your own case using her argument.
3. The Multicultural Library Manifesto asks libraries to "employ staff to reflect the diversity of the community." Annotate this against Hathcock's finding that attrition, not recruitment, is the binding constraint. Does the principle survive intact, need modification, or fail?
4. The Ableism Q&A says accessibility is "the responsibility of an organization to anticipate and meet the needs of all." Anticipation is expensive and needs are open-ended. Name one accessibility investment you think a small public library is *not* obligated to make, and defend the line you drew.
5. Mejia and Chiu recommend "selective acceptance of service requests rather than selective refusal." Is this a strategy for surviving an unjust structure or for changing one? Does the distinction matter to the person doing it?
6. The 2025 amendment added "Belonging" to a title that had read "Equity, Diversity, Inclusion" since 2017. Annotate the paragraph defining belonging: name something a library could be held to have failed at under "belonging" that it would have passed under "inclusion."

## 🔗 Connections

- **Scenario:** "Building Inclusive Spaces" asks you to move through a library as a transgender teen seeking safe resources, an undocumented family needing services, and a patron with invisible disabilities. This week supplies the institutional levers each persona meets. For the undocumented family, the ALA interpretation's list of exclusionary policies (identification requirements, fees, deposits) is the concrete failure point, and the Multicultural Library Manifesto supplies the language-access and staffing standard the branch is measured against. For the patron with invisible disabilities, the Ableism Q&A's social model explains why "we accommodate on request" is itself a barrier: a request requires disclosure, and disclosure is exactly what an invisible disability makes costly. For every persona, Mejia and Chiu's intersectionality warns against writing a single-axis character.
- **Final essay:** Hathcock's core move, that a system's procedures encode norms its stated purpose disclaims, transfers almost unaltered to algorithmic systems, which are also defended as neutral and also select for the population their training data already contains. The Ableism Q&A's social model gives a second transfer: an algorithm that works for most and fails at the margins is not a good system with edge cases but a system with a design defect. Pair either with a Block 4 AI ethics reading.
- **Threads forward:** Week 8 carries the same analytic move into knowledge organization, where Drabinski and Bailund et al. find it in catalogs and subject headings, and where Morales, Knowles, and Bourg extend diversity into the more demanding commitment of social justice. Week 9 scales it globally with Britz and the IFLA 2030 Agenda toolkit. Block 4 tests whether these commitments survive contact with automated systems.

## 📚 Going Deeper

Optional, and genuinely optional. These are also good places to find a final-essay angle.

- University of Illinois Library, "Diversity, Equity, Inclusion, and Social Justice in LIS" (https://www.library.illinois.edu/infosci/websites/dei/). A maintained gateway to LIS-specific DEI scholarship, and the fastest route to what has been published since Hathcock on any subtopic here.
- We Here (https://www.weherespace.org/), a community and support space by and for BIPOC library and information workers; the practical instantiation of the mentorship and counter-space infrastructure Hathcock and Mejia and Chiu both call for.
- ALA, "Services to People with Disabilities: An Interpretation of the Library Bill of Rights" (adopted January 28, 2009; amended June 26, 2018 and June 29, 2025) (https://www.ala.org/advocacy/intfreedom/librarybill/interpretations/servicespeopledisabilities). This is the policy document the Ableism Q&A operationalizes, the enforceable version rather than the explanatory one.
- Dali, K., & Charbonneau, D. H. (2024). Academic ableism and the experiences of disabled and neurodiverse Ph.D. students in LIS programs. *Education for Information, 40*(3), 233–266. https://doi.org/10.3233/efi-240001 (applies Hathcock's structural argument to disability inside LIS graduate education, with survey evidence from U.S. and Canadian programs). ---
