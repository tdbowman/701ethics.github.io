## 1. Children's Museum of Pittsburgh & IMLS (2016)

**Making + learning in museums and libraries: A practitioner's guide and framework**

> Children's Museum of Pittsburgh & Institute of Museum and Library Services. (2016). Making + learning in museums and libraries: A practitioner's guide and framework. Institute of Museum and Library Services. https://www.imls.gov/publications/making-learning-museums-and-libraries

**Find it.** https://www.imls.gov/sites/default/files/publications/documents/makinglearning-museumslibraries-practitionersguideframework.pdf (open access)

**In the course project.** `MuseumsLibrariesMakerspacesPub_v15.pdf`

Produced under a cooperative agreement (IMLS National Leadership Grant MG-00-14-0074-14) between the Children's Museum of Pittsburgh and IMLS, led by Peter Wardrip and Lisa Brahms with Christopher Reich and Tim Carrigan, and developed through more than 50 interviews, 30 site visits, and a January 2015 national convening with thought partners from the Exploratorium, Chicago Public Library, NC State University Libraries, and the Maker Education Initiative.

Its content is three elements. **Purpose**: why making, how it connects to mission, who the audience is, how success is defined. **People**: who facilitates, what the staffing structure is, what capacity exists — the report insists that people "have the potential to make or break a makerspace program." **Pieces and Parts**: tools, materials, and architecture chosen in service of purpose rather than the reverse. The framework's central move is sequencing: practitioners "ought to first ask, 'Why a makerspace?' and not 'What do I need to buy?'" — a polite way of saying most makerspaces were purchased before they were designed.

Two further pieces matter. The report catalogs the goals institutions actually claim — 21st-century skills, workforce development, college and career readiness, STEM/STEAM, seeding entrepreneurship, dispositional shifts like persistence and self-efficacy — which is itself a document of what the maker movement was recruited to justify. And it offers, as an illustrative model of learning, the Children's Museum of Pittsburgh's seven Learning Practices of Making, developed in MAKESHOP with the New York Hall of Science: Inquire; Tinker; Seek and Share Resources; Hack and Repurpose; Express Intention; Develop Fluency; Simplify to Complexify. These are offered as observable behaviors — an assessment instrument for something notoriously resistant to assessment.

Where it lands: genuinely useful and studiedly non-prescriptive, declining to tell anyone what to build. That neutrality is also its limit. It asks "what are the underlying values of your program?" without proposing any, so a makerspace organized entirely around entrepreneurship and one organized around community repair both pass cleanly.

:::{tip} Why it's here
It is the working design instrument for library makerspaces and the block's clearest example of a framework that shapes practice while claiming only to prompt reflection. Its seven Learning Practices are also a real attempt to define observable evidence of learning — precisely the kind of instrument the Block 5 integrator will apply to you.
:::

> "Thus, to create the conditions for learning in these spaces, maker educational professionals ought to first ask, 'Why a makerspace?' and not 'What do I need to buy?'" (Conclusion, p. 53)

## 2. Melo & Nichols (2020)

**Re-making the library makerspace: Critical theories, reflections, and practices**

> Melo, M., & Nichols, J. T. (Eds.). (2020). Re-making the library makerspace: Critical theories, reflections, and practices. Library Juice Press.

**Find it.** No open-access edition; ISBN 978-1-63400-081-9. Check Canvas/Perusall for licensed excerpts and the catalog for the ebook.

:::{warning} Verification note
NEW for Fall 2026 — replaces Fleming (2015) on OER/makerspaces. Verified: editors Maggie Melo and Jennifer T. Nichols, Library Juice Press, 2020, ix + 316 pp., ISBN-13 978-1-63400-081-9 (reviewed in Public Services Quarterly, 17(3)). Correction to the reconstructed list: this is a critical makerspace collection, not an OER text — the substitution changes the topic, not just the citation. STATUS: contents unverified — the volume was not read in preparing this chapter, so no chapter-level claims are made. Read the assigned chapters in Perusall; treat what follows as orientation only.
:::

The premise is in the title. *Re-making* signals that the makerspace as libraries received it — a set of tools, a vocabulary of innovation, a funding rationale — is not a natural object but a construction with a history, and that constructions can be undone and rebuilt. The volume gathers critical theory, practitioner reflection, and practice, deliberately refusing the split in which theory critiques and practice implements.

What a critical turn of this kind typically opens, and what to read for, is the gap between the maker movement's stated universality and its actual demographics and defaults. Who is imagined as a "maker"? The Making + Learning framework records a maker educator asking himself "Okay cool, but am I a maker?" — and if a professional asks that, the question is doing real exclusionary work on patrons. Whose skills count as making, when sewing, cooking, repair, and craft historically coded as women's or working-class labor sit next to 3D printing in the same room but not in the same funding pitch? What does it mean that the goals IMLS catalogs lean so heavily toward workforce development and entrepreneurship — toward preparing people for a labor market rather than toward anything a community named for itself?

Read this against the framework, not after it. The framework asks you to identify your program's values; this collection argues that values in this domain are rarely chosen freshly and are usually inherited from a movement with commitments you did not sign up for — the same argument Cox made about solutionism and Padilla made about monoculture, arriving in a different room.

:::{tip} Why it's here
A block about emerging technology needs at least one text whose method is to interrogate the enthusiasm rather than manage it, and the makerspace is where "innovation" is most visibly a value claim wearing equipment.
:::

## 3. Library makerspace codes of conduct and use policies [primary source set]: Dominican University WeatherTech Innovation Lab (n.d.)

**https://research.dom.edu/innovationlab; University of Minnesota Libraries**

> Library makerspace codes of conduct and use policies [primary source set]: Dominican University WeatherTech Innovation Lab. (n.d.). https://research.dom.edu/innovationlab; University of Minnesota Libraries. (n.d.). Makerspaces code of conduct. https://www.lib.umn.edu/about/makerspace-use; University of Maryland Libraries. (n.d.). Makerspace policies. https://www.lib.umd.edu/visit/tech/makerspace/makerspace-policies; Syracuse University. (n.d.). MakerSpace policies. https://makerspace.syr.edu/about-us/policies

**Find it.** All openly available at the URLs above. Dominican's 3D Print Policy sits within the Innovation Lab guide: https://research.dom.edu/c.php?g=1147226&p=10638398

These are the artifacts, not readings in the usual sense. Read comparatively, they show a profession improvising governance under time pressure, and the improvisations diverge revealingly.

On **weapons**, all three verified policies restrict, at different widths. Minnesota is narrow and functional: "We will not 3D print or otherwise fabricate any kind of part for use in actual functional weaponry," directing uncertain users to consult staff first. Maryland is broader, refusing projects that "look like, function as, or appear to function as real weapons or parts thereof." Syracuse is broadest: "Weapons, weapon accessories, and replicas are banned." That progression — functional, resembling, replica — is a live intellectual-freedom question. A theater prop is banned at one institution and permitted at another.

On **content**, Maryland goes furthest, refusing projects containing "symbols or words that can be deemed offensive or inappropriate by members of the campus community." Set that beside the Library Bill of Rights and Block 1's work on selection versus censorship. Is fabrication a service, where refusal is like declining to notarize, or expression, where refusal is prior restraint? The policies do not answer; they decide.

On **copyright**, Minnesota is most explicit, binding users to laws "including those around copyright and intellectual property" and specifying that equipment may not be used to reproduce copyright-protected materials except for personal use. Maryland prohibits projects that "potentially violate the intellectual property rights of another." Syracuse does not address IP ownership directly, referring users to the university computing policy. Notice that all three place the copyright burden on the patron while the institution owns the machine — the same allocation vendors attempt with AI outputs.

On **commerce, access, and discretion**, all three ban commercial use or mass production, restrict access to institutional affiliates, require training or staff approval, and reserve broad staff discretion. Minnesota requires adult supervision under 18; Syracuse requires an Equipment Use Agreement, PPE, and staff approval before any project, and excludes coursework; Maryland ties laser-cutting hours to fume management and permits only PLA overnight. The discretion clauses are where the real policy lives: every one of these documents ultimately says a staff member may say no.

:::{tip} Why it's here
You will write, apply, or be governed by documents exactly like these. Comparing four teaches what no article can: professional values do not arrive as principles, they arrive as sentences somebody had to write on a Tuesday, and those sentences allocate risk.
:::

> "We will not 3D print or otherwise fabricate any kind of part for use in actual functional weaponry." (University of Minnesota Libraries, *Makerspaces code of conduct*)

## 4. IFLA (2025)

**IFLA statement on copyright and artificial intelligence (AI)**

> International Federation of Library Associations and Institutions. (2025, April 29). IFLA statement on copyright and artificial intelligence (AI). IFLA. https://www.ifla.org/news/realising-potential-supporting-users-ifla-statement-on-copyright-and-artificial-intelligence/ Library Copyright Alliance. (2023, July 10). Library Copyright Alliance principles for copyright and artificial intelligence. https://www.librarycopyrightalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/AI-principles.pdf

**Find it.** IFLA: https://www.ifla.org/news/realising-potential-supporting-users-ifla-statement-on-copyright-and-artificial-intelligence/ and https://repository.ifla.org/items/e9f1f982-7876-46ed-91f9-e508a60cda72 (open access) LCA: https://www.librarycopyrightalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/AI-principles.pdf (open access; also https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/scholcom/392/)

**LCA** argues from confidence in existing US law. Its seven principles: (1) the existing Copyright Act, as applied by the Copyright Office and courts, is fully capable of addressing copyright and AI without amendment; (2) based on well-established precedent, ingesting copyrighted works to create large language models or other AI training databases generally is fair use; (3) because tens or hundreds of millions of works are ingested, any one work's contribution is de minimis, so "remuneration for ingestion is neither appropriate nor feasible"; (4) copyright owners can nonetheless use technical means such as the Robots Exclusion Protocol to prevent training use; (5) an AI output substantially similar in protected expression to an ingested work infringes it; (6) if the original was registered before the infringement, its owner can seek statutory damages against both the AI provider and the user who prompted the output; (7) applying traditional principles of human authorship, an AI-generated work may be copyrightable if the user's prompts sufficiently controlled the output that the result constitutes an original work of human authorship. LCA adds that societal disruption from AI should be met by economy-wide solutions rather than through copyright law.

Follow that structure, because it is elegant and consequential. Liability moves entirely to the output stage: training is free, copying is not. That protects text and data mining, research corpora, and library digitization — and it also protects the largest commercial model developers, an objection LCA anticipates in principle 3 and answers with feasibility rather than justice.

**IFLA** argues from a federation whose members do not all live under fair use. It advocates limitations and exceptions enabling text and data mining of legitimately acquired or accessed content, notes that some countries already have laws flexible enough to permit new technologies, and encourages libraries to make collections AI-ready with appropriate licenses while respecting FAIR and CARE principles — the latter bringing Indigenous data governance directly into a copyright conversation. It endorses the ICOLC statement on AI and licensing. And it makes the move that most repays discussion in an ethics course: it recommends looking beyond copyright law for solutions, warning that copyright should not be used as a blunt force tool for addressing ethical issues, while recommending capacity building, ethical choices in selecting AI tools, and developing guidance and monitoring capacity.

Both are library positions and both are permissive on training, but they locate the remedy differently. LCA says the law already works and the disruption belongs to other policy instruments. IFLA says the law needs exceptions in many countries and that the ethical problems are real but are not copyright's to solve. Ask which framing better serves an author whose work was ingested — then ask whether that is the question either document was written to answer.

:::{tip} Why it's here
The block began with AI as an ethics problem and must end with it as a property problem. These two documents let you watch the profession advocate with real stakes and a real risk of being wrong. ARL's Principle 6 cites LCA directly; reading IFLA against it shows that a "library position" on AI and copyright is a jurisdictional artifact, not a universal truth.
:::

> "Because tens—if not hundreds—of millions of works are ingested to create an LLM, the contribution of any one work to the operation of the LLM is de minimis; accordingly, remuneration for ingestion is neither appropriate nor feasible." (LCA, Principle 3)
