## 1. Children's Museum of Pittsburgh & IMLS (2016)

**Making + learning in museums and libraries: A practitioner's guide and framework**

> Children's Museum of Pittsburgh & Institute of Museum and Library Services. (2016). Making + learning in museums and libraries: A practitioner's guide and framework. Institute of Museum and Library Services. https://www.imls.gov/publications/making-learning-museums-and-libraries

**Find it.** https://www.imls.gov/sites/default/files/publications/documents/makinglearning-museumslibraries-practitionersguideframework.pdf (open access)

**In the course project.** `MuseumsLibrariesMakerspacesPub_v15.pdf`

Produced under a cooperative agreement (IMLS National Leadership Grant MG-00-14-0074-14) between the Children's Museum of Pittsburgh and IMLS, led by Peter Wardrip and Lisa Brahms with Christopher Reich and Tim Carrigan, and developed through more than 50 interviews and site visits (the team visited 30 sites) and a January 2015 national convening, with thought partners including the Exploratorium, Chicago Public Library, NC State University Libraries, and the Maker Education Initiative.

Its content is three elements. **Purpose**: why making, how it connects to mission, who the audience is, how success is defined. **People**: who facilitates, what the staffing structure is, what capacity exists. The report insists that people "have the potential to make or break a makerspace program." **Pieces and Parts**: tools, materials, and architecture chosen in service of purpose rather than the reverse. The framework's central move is sequencing: practitioners "ought to first ask, 'Why a makerspace?' and not 'What do I need to buy?'" That is a polite way of saying most makerspaces were purchased before they were designed.

Two further pieces matter. The report catalogs the goals institutions actually claim: 21st-century skills, workforce development, college and career readiness, STEM/STEAM, seeding entrepreneurship, dispositional shifts like persistence and self-efficacy. That catalog is itself a document of what the maker movement was recruited to justify. And it offers, as an illustrative model of learning, the Children's Museum of Pittsburgh's seven Learning Practices of Making, developed in MAKESHOP with the New York Hall of Science: Inquire; Tinker; Seek and Share Resources; Hack and Repurpose; Express Intention; Develop Fluency; Simplify to Complexify. These are offered as observable behaviors, an assessment instrument for something notoriously resistant to assessment.

Where it lands: genuinely useful and studiedly non-prescriptive, declining to tell anyone what to build. That neutrality is also its limit. It asks "what are the underlying values of your program?" without proposing any, so a makerspace organized entirely around entrepreneurship and one organized around community repair both pass cleanly.

:::{tip} Why it's here
This is the working design instrument for library makerspaces, and the block's clearest example of a framework that shapes practice while claiming only to prompt reflection. Its seven Learning Practices are also a real attempt to define observable evidence of learning, precisely the kind of instrument the Block 5 integrator will apply to you.
:::

> "Thus, to create the conditions for learning in these spaces, maker educational professionals ought to first ask, 'Why a makerspace?' and not 'What do I need to buy?'" (Conclusion, p. 53)

## 2. Marshall & Melo (2019)

**From needs analysis to power analysis: A framework to examine & broker power in makerspaces**

> Marshall, B., & Melo, M. (2019). From needs analysis to power analysis: A framework to examine & broker power in makerspaces. In M. Melo & J. T. Nichols (Eds.), Re-making the library makerspace: Critical theories, reflections, and practices (pp. 83–97). Library Juice Press.

**Find it.** Chapter 4 of the ebook, through the Dominican University library catalog; no open-access edition. The chapter is in Perusall.

:::{note} About the date
The DU ebook shows © 2019, Litwin Books (Library Juice Press is a Litwin imprint); some records list the volume as 2020. Cite the date on the copy you use. Fall 2026 assigns this one chapter rather than the whole volume.
:::

Brianna Marshall, who oversees the Creat'R Lab makerspace at the University of California, Riverside Library, and Maggie Melo, one of the volume's two editors and a faculty member at UNC Chapel Hill's School of Information and Library Science, offer a tool rather than a study. The chapter starts from a workshop at the Racial Equity Institute in Durham, North Carolina, where the instructor, Suzanne Plihcik, asked participants to stop equating the collection of needs analyses with the achievement of inclusion and equity. Asking what women need in a makerspace, or which workshops would attract transgender users, assumes that identifying needs is enough. Systemic change, the authors argue, "intervenes at the structural level of the environment as well: the policies; the hiring; the training; and the arrangement of the space" (p. 86). Hence the thesis: "We should move from needs analyses, to power analyses."

Their definition of power comes from Emily Drabinski, whom you read in Week 8: power produces order, facilitating "some ways of knowing and not others" (p. 85). Their premise comes from Meredith Farkas: library spaces are not neutral, and in an unequal world neutrality upholds inequality. Both authors write from inside makerspaces they helped run. Melo recalls drafting the first purchase list for a makerspace at the University of Arizona. When she suggested sewing machines, a colleague asked, "Don't you think having sewing machines would make men feel excluded from the space?" (p. 88). The question she took from that moment is the chapter's question: how does a learning environment signal who belongs in it?

The framework has four domains, each with an exercise, guiding questions, and suggestions. **People**: sketch a power flow network, place yourself in it, and ask who funded the space, whether decisions are made top down, bottom up, or laterally, and, of any code of conduct, "who compiled it, who enforces it, and what process do they follow?" (p. 90). **Space and equipment**: photograph the space on an average day ("Don't tidy up"), then ask what a new user sees at the threshold, which equipment sits front and center, whose projects are on display, and whether tools can be reached from a seated position. **Events and programming**: who is invited to facilitate, who does the inviting, and whether facilitators are paid. **Outputs**: whether the organization claims intellectual property rights over what is made in the space, and how that is communicated to users. The authors call this "structured, not prescriptive, guidance," say there is no single place to start because power is multi-directional, and recommend that several people in a community run the analysis together (p. 84).

Where it lands: a well-built instrument that the authors candidly describe as nascent; when they wrote, they had applied it only informally. Read it against Making + Learning. Both decline to prescribe, and both put people early. The difference is the starting assumption. Making + Learning assumes a purpose can be chosen freshly; Marshall and Melo assume the space already has a politics, carried by its funding, its purchase list, its signage, and its code of conduct. That is the same argument Cox made about solutionism and Padilla made about monoculture, arriving in a different room.

:::{tip} Why it's here
It turns the critique of the maker movement into something you can do on Monday, and its questions travel. "Who compiled it, who enforces it, and what process do they follow?" works on a makerspace code of conduct, on a vendor contract, and on the Block 5 integrator's report about you.
:::

> "We should move from needs analyses, to power analyses." (p. 86)

## 3. Library makerspace codes of conduct and use policies [primary source set]: Dominican University WeatherTech Innovation Lab (n.d.)

**https://research.dom.edu/innovationlab; University of Minnesota Libraries**

> Library makerspace codes of conduct and use policies [primary source set]: Dominican University WeatherTech Innovation Lab. (n.d.). https://research.dom.edu/innovationlab; University of Minnesota Libraries. (n.d.). Makerspaces code of conduct. https://www.lib.umn.edu/about/makerspace-use; University of Maryland Libraries. (n.d.). Makerspace policies. https://www.lib.umd.edu/visit/tech/makerspace/makerspace-policies; Syracuse University. (n.d.). MakerSpace policies. https://makerspace.syr.edu/about-us/policies

**Find it.** All openly available at the URLs above. Dominican's 3D Print Policy sits within the Innovation Lab guide: https://research.dom.edu/c.php?g=1147226&p=10638398

These are the artifacts, not readings in the usual sense. Read comparatively, they show a profession improvising governance under time pressure, and the improvisations diverge revealingly.

On **weapons**, all three verified policies restrict, at different widths. Minnesota is narrow and functional: "We will not 3D print or otherwise fabricate any kind of part for use in actual functional weaponry," directing uncertain users to consult staff first. Maryland is broader, refusing projects that "look like, function as, or appear to function as real weapons or parts thereof." Syracuse is broadest, listing among its prohibited uses the "production of weapons, weapon accessories, or weapon replicas of any kind." That progression from functional to resembling to replica is a live intellectual-freedom question. A theater prop is banned at one institution and permitted at another.

On **content**, Maryland goes furthest, refusing projects containing "symbols or words that can be deemed offensive or inappropriate by members of the campus community." Set that beside the Library Bill of Rights and Block 1's work on selection versus censorship. Is fabrication a service, where refusal is like declining to notarize, or expression, where refusal is prior restraint? The policies do not answer; they decide.

On **copyright**, Minnesota is most explicit, binding users to laws "including those around copyright and intellectual property" and specifying that equipment may not be used to reproduce copyright-protected materials except for personal use. Maryland prohibits projects that "potentially violate the intellectual property rights of another." Syracuse does not address IP ownership directly, referring users to the university computing policy. Notice that all three place the copyright burden on the patron while the institution owns the machine, the same allocation vendors attempt with AI outputs.

On **commerce, access, and discretion**, all three ban commercial use, and Minnesota also bans mass production. Access differs: Minnesota's Walter Library makerspace is open to everyone during building hours (its Health Sciences Library space is limited to U-Card holders), Maryland is limited to university affiliates, and Syracuse to its own and SUNY ESF communities. Minnesota requires adult supervision for patrons under 18; Syracuse requires "prior approval from a supervising staff member" before beginning any project or operating any machine; Maryland allows only PLA prints overnight and schedules acrylic laser jobs after 4 p.m. because the fumes disrupt the classrooms below. The discretion clauses are where the real policy lives, though they take different forms: Maryland reserves "the right to refuse any projects at the discretion of library staff," Syracuse routes every project through staff approval, and Minnesota asks anyone unsure whether a project is allowable to check with staff.

:::{tip} Why it's here
You will write, apply, or be governed by documents exactly like these. Comparing four teaches what no article can: professional values do not arrive as principles, they arrive as sentences somebody had to write on a Tuesday, and those sentences allocate risk.
:::

> "We will not 3D print or otherwise fabricate any kind of part for use in actual functional weaponry." (University of Minnesota Libraries, *Makerspaces code of conduct*)

## 4. IFLA (2025)

**IFLA statement on copyright and artificial intelligence (AI)**

> International Federation of Library Associations and Institutions. (2025, April 29). IFLA statement on copyright and artificial intelligence (AI). IFLA. https://www.ifla.org/news/realising-potential-supporting-users-ifla-statement-on-copyright-and-artificial-intelligence/ Library Copyright Alliance. (2023, July 10). Library Copyright Alliance principles for copyright and artificial intelligence. https://www.librarycopyrightalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/AI-principles.pdf

**Find it.** IFLA: https://www.ifla.org/news/realising-potential-supporting-users-ifla-statement-on-copyright-and-artificial-intelligence/ and https://repository.ifla.org/items/e9f1f982-7876-46ed-91f9-e508a60cda72 (open access) LCA: https://www.librarycopyrightalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/AI-principles.pdf (open access; also https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/scholcom/392/)

**LCA** argues from confidence in existing US law. Its principles are an opening statement followed by six points, numbered here for discussion: (1) the existing Copyright Act, as applied by the Copyright Office and courts, is fully capable of addressing copyright and AI without amendment; (2) based on well-established precedent, ingesting copyrighted works to create large language models or other AI training databases generally is fair use; (3) because tens or hundreds of millions of works are ingested, any one work's contribution is de minimis, so "remuneration for ingestion is neither appropriate nor feasible"; (4) copyright owners can nonetheless use technical means such as the Robots Exclusion Protocol to prevent training use; (5) an AI output substantially similar in protected expression to an ingested work infringes it; (6) if the original was registered before the infringement, its owner can seek statutory damages against both the AI provider and the user who prompted the output; (7) applying traditional principles of human authorship, an AI-generated work may be copyrightable if the user's prompts sufficiently controlled the AI that the result constitutes an original work of human authorship. LCA adds that responses to AI's broader societal disruption "should be developed on an economy-wide basis" rather than through copyright law.

Follow that structure, because it is elegant and consequential. Liability moves entirely to the output stage: training is free, copying is not. That protects text and data mining, research corpora, and library digitization. It also protects the largest commercial model developers, an objection LCA anticipates in principle 3 and answers with feasibility rather than justice.

**IFLA** argues from a federation whose members do not all live under fair use. It advocates limitations and exceptions enabling text and data mining of legitimately acquired or accessed content, notes that some countries already have laws flexible enough to permit new technologies, and encourages libraries to make collections AI-ready with appropriate licenses while respecting FAIR and CARE principles, the latter bringing Indigenous data governance directly into a copyright conversation. It endorses the ICOLC statement on AI and licensing. And it makes the move that most repays discussion in an ethics course: it recommends looking beyond copyright law for solutions, warning that copyright should not be used as a blunt force tool for addressing ethical issues, while recommending capacity building, ethical choices in selecting AI tools, and developing guidance and monitoring capacity.

Both are library positions and both are permissive on training, but they locate the remedy differently. LCA says the law already works and the disruption belongs to other policy instruments. IFLA says the law needs exceptions in many countries and that the ethical problems are real but are not copyright's to solve. Ask which framing better serves an author whose work was ingested, then ask whether that is the question either document was written to answer.

:::{tip} Why it's here
The block began with AI as an ethics problem and must end with it as a property problem. These two documents let you watch the profession advocate with real stakes and a real risk of being wrong. ARL's Principle 6 cites LCA directly; reading IFLA against it shows that a "library position" on AI and copyright is a jurisdictional artifact, not a universal truth.
:::

> "Because tens—if not hundreds—of millions of works are ingested to create an LLM, the contribution of any one work to the operation of the LLM is de minimis; accordingly, remuneration for ingestion is neither appropriate nor feasible." (LCA, Principle 3)
