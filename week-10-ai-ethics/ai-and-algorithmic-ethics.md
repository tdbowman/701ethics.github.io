# Week 10: AI & Algorithmic Ethics 🤖

**Block 4: Emerging Technologies & Future Ethics | October 26 – November 1, 2026**

---

*An algorithm does not read the Library Bill of Rights. It reads data.*

You have spent nine weeks learning to reason from professional values. This week those values meet a technology that does not care about them. An algorithm does not read the Library Bill of Rights; it reads data. When a library buys, licenses, or builds an AI system, it quietly delegates value judgments (about what counts as relevant, what counts as offensive, what counts as a good patron) to a process optimized for something else.

The five texts answer the same question at five altitudes, and the gaps between them are where the work happens. At the top sits the ALA's *Guidance on the Use of Artificial Intelligence in Libraries*, adopted by Council in July 2026. It is new this year, and it anchors this block. Just below, ARL's *Research Libraries Guiding Principles for Artificial Intelligence* (2024): seven principles written substantially as a policy-advocacy platform. Then ACRL's *AI Competencies for Academic Library Workers* (2025), which translates values into things an individual worker should be able to do. Then Cox (2022), who argues that codes at any altitude are too abstract to help in the moment and offers eight concrete scenarios instead. Finally Mannheimer et al. (2024), who examined 89 case studies of what libraries actually did and found that most barely discussed ethics.

### 🎯 What You'll Learn

- Read the new ALA AI guidance against ARL's principles and ACRL's competencies
- Use Cox's eight scenarios as a method for locating harm before it arrives
- Confront Mannheimer's finding that guidance and practice have been two separate conversations
- Build the vocabulary you need to argue with an algorithm's judgment of you

### 🧠 Big Questions to Consider

- If a code is, as Cox argues, too high-level to resolve a specific dilemma, what work is it actually doing, and what would it mean for the ALA's 2026 guidance to "succeed"?
- Mannheimer et al. found that accuracy and human-in-the-loop dominated practitioner ethics talk. When does a technical criterion function as an ethical alibi?
- ARL asserts copyright law is "flexible and robust enough" for AI; ALA asserts libraries should not replace service roles with chatbots. Which is a claim about the world and which is a commitment about ourselves? Does the difference change how you would argue against it?
