## 1. ALA (2026)

**Guidance on the use of artificial intelligence in libraries**

> American Library Association. (2026). Guidance on the use of artificial intelligence in libraries. American Library Association. https://www.ala.org/tools/standards-and-guidelines/guidance-use-artificial-intelligence-libraries

**Find it.** https://www.ala.org/tools/standards-and-guidelines/guidance-use-artificial-intelligence-libraries (open access). Council document PDF: https://www.ala.org/sites/default/files/2026-06/ALA%20CD%2044.2%20AI%20Guidance%20Document%20-%20Final.pdf

The guidance rests on a structural claim: AI is not a new domain requiring new values but a new pressure on values libraries already hold. Its central section, "Library Values and AI," works through six — Public Good, Intellectual Freedom, Privacy, Sustainability, Diversity/Equity/Inclusion/Accessibility, and Labor — asking in each case what AI does in practice. Public good is framed as documented public-service purposes with preserved human decision-making. Intellectual freedom is framed in terms of ranking: automated systems threaten it when they obscure how information is ranked. That relocates the censorship concern from removal to ordering. Nothing is banned by a recommender; things are simply never surfaced.

Two of the six are ones you likely have not met in an earlier code. Sustainability treats environmental lifecycle — energy use, data-center emissions, electronic waste, resource extraction — as a professional ethics question and pushes it into procurement, asking that vendors disclose carbon estimates, data-center energy sources, and water usage as a condition of evaluation. Labor is where the document is bluntest, stating that libraries should not replace reference, readers' advisory, instructional services, or community support roles with AI recommenders or chatbots. That is a line, and national associations rarely draw them.

Privacy extends confidentiality into data governance: patron data, institutional data, local history, staff workflows, and usage logs should remain under library, public-sector, or community control — a claim about sovereignty, not just non-disclosure. An appendix supplies an AI Ethics Framework of six principles: the Common Good of Humanity and the Planet; the No-Harm Principle, Privacy, and Protection; Human Dignity; Responsibility and Explainability; Democracy; and Equity, Social Justice and Working Towards a Better Future. Where it lands: this is a policy-drafting instrument, not an adjudication instrument. It tells a library what its policy should be about; it does not tell a librarian what to do at 4:15 on a Tuesday when a vendor demo is going well.

:::{tip} Why it's here
It is the anchor because it is the first time the profession's largest association has said, in its own voice and with Council behind it, what AI does to library values — and because it was adopted three months before you read it. You are not studying a settled consensus but watching one form, with the seams still visible where a working group had to choose between a commitment and a hedge.
:::

> "Libraries should not replace reference, readers' advisory, instructional services, or community support roles with AI recommender systems or AI chatbots." (Labor section)

## 2. ARL (2024)

**Research libraries guiding principles for artificial intelligence**

> Association of Research Libraries. (2024). Research libraries guiding principles for artificial intelligence. Association of Research Libraries. https://www.arl.org/resources/research-libraries-guiding-principles-for-artificial-intelligence/

**Find it.** https://www.arl.org/resources/research-libraries-guiding-principles-for-artificial-intelligence/ (open access; published April 2024)

**In the course project.** `ARLResearchLibrariesGuidingPrinciplesforArtificialIntelligence.pdf`

Three pages, seven principles, and an explicit purpose: ARL says it will rely on these in policy advocacy and engagement. The seven are: (1) libraries democratize access to AI tools and technology to foster digital literacy among all people; (2) libraries commit to understanding where distortions and biases are present in AI models and applications; (3) libraries champion transparency and information integrity, advocating for openness in algorithms, training data, and methodologies used to create AI technologies they license; (4) libraries believe "no human, no AI"; (5) libraries prioritize the security and privacy of users in the use of AI tools, technology, and training data; (6) libraries assert that copyright law in the US and Canada is flexible and robust enough to respond to many copyright issues arising at the intersection of technology and AI; and (7) libraries negotiate to preserve the scholarly use of digital information, asserting that licenses should not restrict scholarly use.

Two features repay attention. Principle 2 pairs "distortions" with "biases" and grounds itself in inevitability — it recognizes the inherent susceptibility of all AI to distortion and positions library expertise as raising user awareness rather than repairing models. That is modest, defensible, and slightly deflationary: the library's job is literacy, not repair. Principles 6 and 7 are where the document stops being about ethics and becomes about leverage. Principle 6 is a legal position identical to the Library Copyright Alliance's, which ARL cites alongside CARL; Principle 7 is a bargaining posture aimed at license terms, supported by the resource list's pointer to the ICOLC Statement on AI in Licensing (March 2024) and Michael Ridley's work on explainable AI and the "right to an explanation." ARL is arguing that research libraries should not seek new law but should use existing law and contract negotiation aggressively. Whether that is confident or complacent is live — and Week 12's IFLA/LCA pairing reopens it.

:::{tip} Why it's here
It gives you a compact, quotable set of principles from a specific institutional standpoint and lets you practice reading a values document as a political document. Compared to ALA's six values, the absences are not oversights: ARL has no labor principle and no sustainability principle. That is what each organization is for.
:::

> "Libraries believe 'no human, no AI.' This principle underscores the importance of human involvement in critical decision-making junctures, enhancing accountability, ethical considerations, and the overall reliability of AI-driven processes within the research environment." (Principle 4, p. 2)

## 3. Cox (2022)

**The ethics of AI for information professionals: Eight scenarios**

> Cox, A. (2022). The ethics of AI for information professionals: Eight scenarios. Journal of the Australian Library and Information Association, 71(3), 201–214. https://doi.org/10.1080/24750158.2022.2084885

**Find it.** https://doi.org/10.1080/24750158.2022.2084885 (open access). The scenarios themselves: https://doi.org/10.15131/shef.data.17081138.v1 (CC BY-SA)

**In the course project.** `Cox2022The Ethics of AI for Information Professionals  Eight Scenarios.pdf`

Cox begins from a limitation: professional ethics codes "are too high level to offer precise or complete guidance" (p. 201). His answer is scenarios — concrete, second-person, deliberately unresolved. Along the way he insists AI is not only a technology but "a set of commercial applications and a related industrial complex," invoking Crawford and Joler's *Anatomy of an AI System* to show that a voice assistant depends on user data, exploited click work, raw-material extraction, and vast energy. He distills the ethics literature — Jobin et al.'s 84 codes, Fjeld et al.'s eight themes, Floridi and Cowls's five principles — into five issues of his own: bias; transparency, explainability and accountability; privacy; safety and security; and human choice.

The sharpest section concerns the ethics of the AI *industry*. Cox marshals the critique that published ethics codes may be "ethics washing" motivated by a desire to avoid regulation, that design decisions get ethical review while the business practices driving them do not, and that ethically developed code can still be applied unethically. He notes that AI is biased partly because its builders are unrepresentative, and flags a finding that should unsettle you: Ferguson et al.'s RFID study suggests information professionals may be over-reliant on vendors to ensure a technology is ethical.

The eight scenarios, shared under CC BY-SA and revised after expert feedback, are: (1) *Supporting first responders* — objections to sharing data for an emergency system; (2) *Nudges* — supplying data to a tool that nudges students toward wellbeing; (3) *The voice assistant* — a public voice-assistant service; (4) *A special collection* — a donation predicated on access to controversial content; (5) *Forum moderation* — automated moderation of a public forum; (6) *The recommender system* — reactions to recommendations based on past reading; (7) *Stakeholders* — involving communities in an AI project; (8) *Project partners* — power and ethics in a joint project. Cox maps each to issues: scenario 3 raises jobs, bias, and sexism; scenario 6 raises chilling effects, transparency, privacy, and commodification; scenario 8 raises environmental impact. Scenarios 7 and 8 are the ones people skip and shouldn't — they are not about a technology at all but about who is in the room. His implicit thesis is that most real AI ethics failures in libraries will be organizational, not technical.

:::{tip} Why it's here
Block 4's immersive scenario *is* a Cox scenario. He also gives you the vocabulary — chilling effects, commodification, deresponsibilisation, ethics washing — that lets you name what is wrong with a deployment when nothing has technically malfunctioned.
:::

> "Given the vested interests suppliers always have in promoting their products, it is essential that practitioners apply their ethics principles independently." (p. 203)

## 4. Mannheimer et al. (2024)

**Responsible AI practice in libraries and archives: A review of the literature**

> Mannheimer, S., Bond, N., Young, S. W. H., Scates Kettler, H., Marcus, A., Slipher, S. K., Clark, J. A., Shorish, Y., Rossmann, D., & Sheehey, B. (2024). Responsible AI practice in libraries and archives: A review of the literature. Information Technology and Libraries, 43(3). https://doi.org/10.5860/ital.v43i3.17245

**Find it.** https://doi.org/10.5860/ital.v43i3.17245 (open access). Research data: https://doi.org/10.5064/F6QME490

**In the course project.** `Mannheimer2024ResponsibleAIPractice1724520240922galley.pdf`

Ten authors asked how AI is being used in libraries and archives and what ethical concerns surface during implementation, then answered by coding 89 case studies published between 2017 and July 2023, limited to the US and Canada and required to include at least one practitioner author. They used constructivist grounded theory and published their coding data openly.

The descriptive findings first. Implementation is concentrated: 67 case studies came from academic libraries, 63 of them large institutions (10,000+ FTE); only five came from public libraries. The commonest applications were metadata extraction (21), natural language processing (18), image recognition (15), and text mining and machine learning (11 each), grouped into three themes — metadata extraction, recommendations and reference, and assessment.

Then the finding that should reorganize how you read everything else. Just over half the papers (49 of 89, ~55%) mentioned ethics or values at all. The thirteen codes, in frequency order: accuracy (23), human-in-the-loop (16), bias (14), social justice (11), transparency (10), privacy (7), policy and guidelines (6), user communities (5), cost considerations (5), reliability (4), accessibility (3), access (3), consent (1). The authors then make an interpretive move worth arguing with: observing that accuracy and human-in-the-loop are expressed as primarily technical rather than ethical considerations, they remove both and recount. Coverage drops to 31 of 89, roughly 35%. Consent, central to research ethics, appeared once in 89 studies.

Their implications are measured. Bias is "an especially thorny issue" partly mitigable through transparent messaging rather than solvable; a few large academic libraries may be setting the tone for everyone; and as vendors supply more AI products, the profession must advocate harder for its own values against conflicting ones. They note honestly that the review ends mid-2023, before the generative-AI product wave.

:::{tip} Why it's here
It converts a suspicion into a measurement. Anyone can assert that library AI ethics talk is thin; this shows exactly how thin, on what dimensions, with what caveats. It is also the week's best model of how to critique a corpus you belong to.
:::

> "So long as tools are created by (inherently biased) humans and trained on data also created by (inherently biased) humans, there will be no perfect way to remove bias. In other words, removing bias or 'debiasing' is likely an unattainable goal. We can only strive to be transparent about our known biases." (Implications for Responsible AI Practice in Libraries and Archives)

## 5. ACRL (2025)

**AI competencies for academic library workers**

> Association of College and Research Libraries. (2025). AI competencies for academic library workers. Association of College and Research Libraries. https://www.ala.org/acrl/standards/ai

**Find it.** https://www.ala.org/acrl/standards/ai; PDF: https://www.ala.org/sites/default/files/2025-10/acrl_ai_competencies.pdf (open access)

Where ALA and ARL describe what libraries should value, ACRL describes what a worker should be able to do. Four competency areas: Ethical Considerations; Knowledge and Understanding; Analysis and Evaluation; and Use and Application. The ordering is itself an argument — ethics is first, framed as awareness of the broad range of ethical issues connected with the exploration, evaluation, selection, use, and creation of AI tools.

Those ethics competencies push into territory the higher-altitude documents only gesture at. They name the unseen labor embedded in AI systems and ask workers to be aware of it. They name environmental impact and ask workers to advocate for more efficient technologies. They name equitable access concretely — costs of premium services, restrictions in licensing agreements, centralized control of AI technologies and infrastructure — converting "the digital divide" from a demographic fact into a procurement consequence. Bias is traced to data representation gaps and algorithm design; intellectual property is acknowledged as unsettled.

The other three areas build an evaluation pipeline: understand the technology, evaluate a specific tool, apply it to workflows and instruction. This is the most actionable document of the week and the most individualizing: it puts the burden on the worker. Read against Cox's warning that ethics codes focus on individuals rather than organizations and so evade organizational responsibility, ACRL's competency framing looks like both an empowerment and a transfer of risk.

:::{tip} Why it's here
It bridges principle and practice, and it is the document most directly about you — a person about to enter the profession being told what you will be expected to do about AI. It also supplies the evaluation criteria the scenario will demand.
:::

> "Understand how the development and use of AI systems are impacting the environment and advocate for more efficient technologies." (Ethical Considerations)
